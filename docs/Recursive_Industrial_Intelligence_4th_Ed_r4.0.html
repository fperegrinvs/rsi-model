Abstract

This paper develops and tests a conditional thesis: advanced AI, reusable launch, robotics, energy systems, and automated manufacturing could be combined into an industrial system beyond Earth that maintains and expands its own productive capacity. This Fourth Edition retains the complete Third Edition quantitative baseline and expands the object of analysis from an abstract industrial substrate to a reproducible autonomous seed: physical equipment, embodied autonomy, software and simulation, industrial memory, experimental capacity, and element-resolved supply. Part I implements a ten-sector, multisector capacity model with decomposed depreciation, repair, recycling, stockpiles, imports, common-cause shocks, and architecture-specific constraints for LEO, the lunar surface, and Mars. Its unit-invariant replication metric is balanced capacity doubling; its endpoint is realized post-shock growth, and its bottleneck diagnostic is a finite 1% supply-response test with explicit co-binding. The assumption ensembles produce outcomes ranging from collapse to sustained expansion, with electronics dominant on the Moon, electronics and power on Mars, and captured/recovered feed dominant in LEO. The inherited model begins with positive capacity in all ten sectors and omits construction lags, commissioning, yield ramps, and minimum efficient scales; its doubling times are stylized capacity-model first passages, not factory-construction or seed-commissioning forecasts. Part II builds an affine levelized-cost model for terrestrial and space compute, separating integrated disposable satellites from serviceable/reusable platforms. At central inputs, generic LEO compute costs 3.97 times Earth on the serviceable-platform architecture and 4.90 times Earth if integrated and disposable; the disclosed Starmind AI1 mass case remains 2.56 and 2.90 times Earth, respectively. The analysis therefore supports neither a point forecast of autonomous industry nor present orbital-compute parity. It identifies the architecture-dependent measurements and demonstrations that would most change those conclusions. A new 18-sector, 17-material integrated-bootstrap model then asks how much seed mass is sufficient, whether its material graph can close first on the Moon and later across asteroidal sources, and whether it can construct and commission a second production cell without routine human intervention. Under the declared central assumptions, a lunar seed retaining an Earth-supplied material vitamin stock is 216.7 t and commissions a second cell at 11.2 years; the strict lunar-only architecture is infeasible; and a composite Moon–asteroid network is 185.5 t, commissions at 22.8 years, and reaches 12.55 normalized operating cells at year 40. Across 200 deterministic assumption draws per case, respectively 126, 0, and 187 are feasible. Those frequencies characterize only the stipulated ensemble. The model operationalizes the coupled loop and sharpens its material and autonomy conditions; it does not demonstrate that the assumed processes, source portfolio, AI gains, or industrial closure exist.

Keywords: recursive industrial improvement; space manufacturing; industrial closure; orbital compute; lunar industry; multisector capacity; autonomous industrial seed; embodied autonomy; lunar materials; techno-economics; Monte Carlo.

Scope and Status

This is a strategic hypothesis and research program with, now, its first quantitative core — not a techno-economic demonstration. It identifies necessary conditions, bottlenecks, and discriminating tests for a self-expanding industrial system, computes what its own assumptions imply, and states throughout which of its six propositions each conclusion rests on.

The reference system in this edition is machine-only: no resident human population, agriculture, or biosphere-scale life-support loop is required for industrial reproduction. Human settlement can create demand and impose additional requirements, but it is not a prerequisite of the thesis. Water, hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen remain important as propellants, reductants, coolants, lubricants, polymers, process gases, and chemical feedstocks; removing agriculture narrows the closure problem but does not remove volatile chemistry from it.

Six distinct propositions run through this document and carry different evidence: (P1) a recursively expanding industrial system is conceptually possible — a respectable case is made; (P2) such a system might eventually be technically feasible — quantitatively explored for both the inherited industrial substrate and a first explicit integrated bootstrap (Part I): outcomes span failure to sustained expansion across named assumption sets, with the feasibility margin localized to architecture-specific bottlenecks; the coupled intelligence–industry functions now have an operational stylized form, but their coefficients remain assumptions rather than evidence of complete-system feasibility (III.6); (P3a) orbital and lunar compute during the Earth-dependent bridge would be economically competitive — now quantified (Part II) and, at anchored values, negative, with crossover requiring a specific, currently unanchored corner of parameter space; (P3b) a mature, high-closure space-industrial ecology would eventually obtain a structural economic advantage over terrestrial expansion — unquantified: Part II prices the bridge architectures, not an ecology that manufactures its own power systems, radiators, structures, and replacement hardware off Earth, so nothing in this document establishes or refutes P3b (the Fourth Edition couples physical and compute quantities, not the mature ecology’s full costs); (P4a) the companies are deliberately integrating launch, communications, AI, terrestrial and orbital compute, chip design and manufacture, and prospective off-world energy and manufacturing — substantially documented, since SEC-filed offering materials associated with the S-1 (the Rule 433 free-writing prospectus and incorporated materials) name orbital compute, lunar manufacturing and energy, in-orbit manufacturing, manufacturing on the Moon and Mars, asteroid mining, and “interplanetary industrialization” among the initiatives [S27]; and (P4b) that this integration is intended to produce recursive industrial closure — inferential, with no closure program, seed bill of materials, reproduction metric, or minimum-cut roadmap published, and assessed against competing explanations (Part III). The split matters: the two halves carry materially different evidential status, and earlier editions conflated them. Conclusions are licensed by the strongest proposition they rest on, not by the conjunction of all six.

Executive Summary

This document develops a unified hypothesis: the deepest strategic value of advanced AI, reusable launch, energy systems, robotics, and automated manufacturing may lie in combining them into a recursively improving industrial system beyond Earth. The end state is not merely a rocket fleet, a settlement, a humanoid robot, or a frontier model, but a distributed industrial ecology able to sustain itself, expand productive capacity, and progressively reduce Earth dependence.

Central proposition. Once a space-based industrial system can obtain local energy and materials, maintain its machinery, manufacture additional productive equipment, and coordinate itself through AI, the primary constraint shifts from acquiring terrestrial inputs to allocating productive throughput over time.

That proposition distinguishes two growth regimes. An orbital data center whose servers, structures, power systems, and replacements continue to be made on Earth is deployed capacity, not a self-expanding industry:

dCorbitdt=qcLE→O(t)−δCCorbit. \frac{\mathrm d C_{\mathrm{orbit}}}{\mathrm d t} = q_c L_{E\rightarrow O}(t)-\delta_C C_{\mathrm{orbit}}.

At a constant Earth-to-orbit delivery rate LE→OL_{E\rightarrow O}, capacity grows approximately linearly while depreciation is small and ultimately approaches qcLE→O/δCq_cL_{E\rightarrow O}/\delta_C. By contrast, a closed industrial stock that allocates a fraction of its own output to producing additional productive equipment follows the idealized endogenous law

dHdt=(r−δH)H, \frac{\mathrm d H}{\mathrm d t}=(r-\delta_H)H,

and compounds only when r>δHr>\delta_H. Off-Earth location does not create that acceleration: reproductive closure does. The role of off-Earth industry is to combine that endogenous growth regime with access to matter, energy, and usable area outside Earth’s biosphere and society-priced siting constraints. Earth-fed orbital compute can therefore raise intelligence at a constant deployment rate without crossing the industrial-reproduction threshold; the two cases must not be conflated.

The framework contains two coupled loops. In the digital loop, AI improves software, research, engineering, evaluation, and coordination. In the physical loop, AI and robots improve mining, refining, power, manufacturing, construction, transport, and the production of additional compute. Each loop strengthens the other:

better intelligence → better automation → more productive capacity →
more compute → better intelligence

The economic argument compresses to two walls and two escapes. The capital wall: expansion purchased through markets runs into scarcity pricing and supply-elasticity limits as ambitions scale; the escape is self-replication, which re-denominates expansion from dollars into materials, energy, and time. The operating wall: on Earth, society-priced costs — land, permits, politics, waste heat — rise with scale and cannot be closed away; the escape is space, where permission attaches to the gateway rather than the complex. Both escapes pass through one passage: the bridge, in which the first self-replicating seed is bought at full market price, operated where depreciation is most severe, and financed by intermediate products.

This edition prices the passage and models the seed. Four headline results:

  1. The active bottleneck is architecture-specific. A unit-invariant finite-response diagnostic relaxes each material balance by 1%, then divides credit among rows whose positive response is within 5% of the largest. Electronics receives about 94–97% of co-binding credit on the lunar surface; electronics and power divide most credit on Mars; and capture/mining receives about 86–100% in LEO. The LEO architecture has no ore body and depends on captured and recovered feed. Import-cut experiments are small, margin-conditional samples, so their defensible executive-level result is ordinal rather than a precise frequency: import dependency is architecture-dependent, and electronics is often load-bearing where the sampled vitamin gap is deepest. That identity is conditional on a homogeneous electronics-productivity assumption, not a measurement of real electronics closure.
  2. Balanced-capacity doubling and durable expansion are different outcomes. The headline replication metric is λ(t) = min_j K_j(t)/K_j(0) ≥ 2: every modeled sector has doubled in its own capacity units. It is not a claim that a commissioned second physical factory exists. The endpoint records realized, post-shock growth; the pre-shock allocation endpoint remains a diagnostic. In central ensembles, 267/400 LEO, 224/400 lunar, and 60/400 Mars runs are expanding at year 30, but only 122, 20, and 4 respectively sustain positive realized growth throughout years 25–30. Median balanced doubling among runs that reach it is about 26.0 years in LEO, 5.0 on the Moon, and 11.0 on Mars. The Earth-import-cut LEO result is expressly conditional on continued capture feed and is not an autonomous-ecology result.
  3. The space-compute crossover is an architecture story, not a launch-price story. At central inputs, generic LEO compute is 3.97× Earth on a serviceable/reusable platform and 4.90× if the satellite is integrated and disposable. The Starmind AI1 disclosure scenario is 2.56× and 2.90×, respectively. None of the 2,000 shared-draw Monte Carlo runs in any reported architecture/reference cell favors space. Negative median break-even launch prices show why: qualification cost, hardware and platform life, and utilization can dominate launch. The deterministic aspirational case reaches 0.76× only by combining company targets with three favorable, unpublished model bounds and is assigned no probability.
  4. The autonomous seed is a material-and-knowledge graph, not a cargo total. The new 18-sector, 17-material layer requires physical equipment, digital tools, industrial memory, experiments, task-resolved autonomy, gravity-compatible processes, and a commissioned second cell. Under its declared central assumptions, the Earth-vitamin lunar bridge requires 216.7 t of seed including 58.1 t of vitamin stock and commissions the second cell at 11.2 years. Strict lunar-only closure is infeasible because trace inputs cannot reproduce the assumed electronics and process-equipment stack. A deliberately composite Moon–asteroid source closes at 185.5 t, commissions at 22.8 years, and reaches 12.55 normalized operating cells at year 40. Across 200 deterministic assumption draws, the three cases are feasible in 126, 0, and 187 draws; these are ensemble frequencies, not success probabilities.

The Fourth layer also makes one architecture boundary stateful: industrial closure does not license a dense orbital shell. Active, derelict, and fragment stocks feed a last-five-year debris reproduction ratio. In the declared year-40 scenarios, the Earth-fed orbital cases are subcritical (Rdebris=0.61R_{\mathrm{debris}}=0.61 with weak mitigation and 0.35 with strong mitigation), while dense endogenous deployment is supercritical under both (R=5.53R=5.53 and 5.58). A sparse distributed topology reaches the non-supercritical boundary only because its collision-kernel multiplier is explicitly set to 0.01. These are topology stress tests, not orbital traffic forecasts [S45–S49].

The strategic assessment (Part III): the evidence substantially establishes deliberate integrated space–AI–industrial development (P4a) at the level of declared direction and early implementation — the initiative list in the SEC-filed offering materials and the first-party messaging are difficult to explain under a purely terrestrial reading. It does not establish recursive closure as the objective (P4b): the closure-specific observables — seed bills of materials, reproduction metrics, minimum-cut roadmaps, locally reproducible production equipment — remain absent. What has changed is that the discriminating indicators are now model-ranked: the rational next moves of a closure-seeking actor — electronics-closure engineering, loss-tolerant orbital-compute persistence, materials-recovery recycling — are exactly the observations the models identify as informative.

Part I — Technical Feasibility

I.1 Recursive self-improvement: the digital loop

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) occurs when an intelligent system contributes to the process that produces a more capable successor. The useful unit is not a public model release but a verified improvement loop: identify a weakness, propose a change, run an experiment, evaluate, deploy, and use the improved system to accelerate the next cycle.

I(t+1)=I(t)+ΔI(t),ΔI=L×G \begin{aligned} I(t+1) &= I(t) + \Delta I(t), \\ \Delta I &= L \times G \end{aligned}

I is effective intelligence; L is the number of useful loops completed; G is the average gain per successful loop. The loop rate is constrained by the least scalable part of the development system:

L=min⁡{LC(C),LD(D),LX(X),LV(V),LR(R)} L = \min\!\left\{L_C(C), L_D(D), L_X(X), L_V(V), L_R(R)\right\}

— each conversion function turning its resource (compute, data, experimental throughput, verification capacity, deployment reliability) into verified loops per unit time. Efficiency is partly a function of intelligence, E(t) = f(I(t)): more capable systems may improve coding, architecture search, synthetic-data generation, hardware design, factory scheduling, scientific modeling, and evaluation design. The form of f — logarithmic, linear, superlinear, saturating — is unknown; a rigorous analysis identifies which regime observed loop completion supports rather than assuming takeoff. Public releases are lagging indicators; the strategically relevant measure is verified improvement loops per calendar time. The verification term V recurs in a second role as the governing safety constraint (Part III.4). Time-spaced releases nevertheless remain useful: they generate real-world feedback, revenue, reliability data, and operational learning. A release is part of the loop, but it is not the loop.

I.2 Recursive industrial improvement: the physical loop

Digital intelligence depends on a physical substrate: chips, data centers, electricity, cooling, networks, factories, labor, materials, logistics, maintenance — a dependency inventory, not a sum; any term can bind. The physical recursive loop begins when AI and robotics improve the production of those inputs: better intelligence designs and operates better machines; better machines expand energy and manufacturing; expanded industry produces more compute; more compute supports better intelligence.

Energy hardware is not energy: “with solar, energy equals hardware” expresses a recursion, not an identity. Installed arrays generate power that supports production of additional arrays; the system still pays in materials, machine-hours, maintenance, and conversion losses. Robotic labor is manufactured productive capacity: when robots participate in producing factories, energy systems, and additional robots, labor becomes partially reproducible, and the robot reproduction ratio must exceed one after depreciation, failures, and the equipment needed for high-complexity components — stated with the horizon and unit it requires, R_robot,T = discounted lifetime robot-equivalent capacity produced over T ÷ robot-equivalent capacity retired, consumed, or irreversibly committed over T, since a factory is not “consumed” merely because it helped build robots. In the long run the decisive competitive variable — under the threshold-like, small-α branch of the capability model examined in II.5 — is not model quality but the doubling time of productive hardware — and Part I.4 examines what that replication must outrun.

The embodied-autonomy threshold. A mobile manipulator can have enough joints, reach, payload, sensing, and tool interfaces to span many industrial tasks without being able to close the labor loop. Kinematic sufficiency is not cognitive sufficiency. The threshold relevant here is whether the combined robot-and-AI system can diagnose unfamiliar faults, choose and use tools, execute long-horizon construction and repair, recover from failed actions, learn from intervention, and verify that the restored machine is safe to return to service. Present humanoid programs, including Optimus, are evidence of an intended general-purpose hardware form, not evidence that this unscripted industrial task set has been solved. The Fourth Edition therefore treats embodied autonomy as task coverage and recovery capability, not as robot count or degrees of freedom.

The loops couple through a second channel: data as the exhaust of the physical loop. Operating physical industry generates data that exists nowhere else — test telemetry, process parameters, failure archives, fleet traces. It commoditises less readily than public text, for four reasons: negative results (the map of what does not work survives only in the archives of whoever paid for the failures); sim-to-real ground truth (real telemetry is the verification fuel for AI-proposed physical designs); legal fencing (export-controlled data cannot lawfully enter open corpora — and equally cannot feed publicly served models, so the strongest-moat data is harvestable only in an internal, access-controlled variant); and temporal lead time (longitudinal data imposes a minimum calendar-time barrier to a like-for-like archive: a rival beginning today matches the sampling rate but cannot reproduce the historical intervention-and-outcome sequence retroactively, though capital compresses much of the disadvantage through acquisition, larger current fleets, accelerated and parallel experimentation, simulation, transfer learning, and newer processes that make the old archive less relevant [S34] — a lead-time barrier, not an impossibility). The data-closure index κ_D is a direction-of-effort indicator, not a computable ratio: its denominator is not measurable, and value depends on quality, linkage, and integration, not origin. The honest caveat: proprietary physical data is lead time, not permanence — any actor operating comparable fleets and factories generates comparable exhaust, at national scale in China’s case.

The public record supplies two applications of the same supplemental-training mechanism, which is evidence of an emerging pattern rather than proof of a repeatable one. In July 2026, days after an in-house engineering model was reported to have received a supplemental pass over the coding data of an acquired developer-tools company, Musk said that the SpaceX engineering corpus — excluding ITAR-controlled material — would enter supplemental training of its roughly two-trillion-parameter successor [S11–S13]. The relevant advantage is therefore not raw ownership alone:

D_advantage = f(uniqueness, causal linkage, coverage, quality,
                accessibility, integration, verification value)

Generation is insufficient; the data must be integrated and attributable enough to train on. Tesla’s first CIO has described building the custom Warp enterprise system in roughly four months in 2012 after the company rejected off-the-shelf ERP, then extending it continuously so operations could change without waiting on a vendor [S15]. SpaceX independently built its own internal manufacturing and enterprise system, Warp Drive [S16]. The convergence on the same build-don’t-buy pattern is evidence that iteration speed, not a common brand name, drives the architecture.

Commentary describes the resulting ideal as a unified build record for each produced unit: as designed (models and simulations), as built (supplier batches, process settings, torques, and factory tests), and as deployed (fleet telemetry). Integrated cross-functional data and over-the-air learning are independently documented; the exact tri-phase schema is only partially confirmed and is treated as indicative [S14–S17]. Its analytical importance is the causal linkage from design intent through production parameters to field behavior — the quality dimension of κ_D.

For an autonomous seed, this record is more than training data: it is part of the transported productive system. Process recipes, material models, calibration procedures, controller firmware, CAD/CAM files, compilers, simulators, verification suites, dependency mirrors, failure histories, and intervention records determine whether physical equipment can be operated, repaired, and adapted after communication or supply is cut. The manuscript calls this package industrial memory. It is not equivalent to “all industrial knowledge,” an incoherent completion standard; it is a machine-readable operating foundation coupled to the experimental capacity to discover what the initial package omitted.

Scale proxies require disciplined interpretation. By mid-2026 the vehicle fleet had accumulated more than ten billion miles under supervised autonomy, against roughly 220 million fully driverless miles disclosed by another operator [S17, S30]. No valid conversion exists between those regimes, so the figures are not an advantage multiple. More informative disclosures concern structure: an occupancy network trained on 1.4 billion fleet video frames and an auto-labeling system replacing roughly five million hours of manual annotation [S17]. The aerospace analogue is thirteen heavy-lift flight tests by mid-2026: few observations, each with high information content [S31]. These quantities demonstrate data generation and integration capacity, not autonomous industrial closure.

I.3 Why space changes the cost structure

On Earth, useful locations compete with communities, ecosystems, jurisdictions, and alternative uses. Off Earth, the monetary price of unused area approaches zero — but infrastructure does not become free; the cost changes kind, from purchasing scarce terrestrial sites and coordinating human institutions toward transporting, converting, and maintaining matter through an automated production network:

Earth: land + permits + construction + energy + materials + labor + coordination
Space: transport + energy + extraction + refining + manufacturing + maintenance + coordination

The land term declines sharply, but useful positions can remain scarce because of illumination, thermal conditions, communications geometry, orbital stability, resource proximity, political accessibility, and transport energy. The first system must still arrive, survive, obtain or import matter, reject heat, maintain operations, and replace failed equipment. Space removes neither scarcity nor cost; it changes their composition.

The standard external objections — station-keeping, collision management, imported mass, maintenance, coordination — become internal product categories once the complex can manufacture the required systems: propellant lines, tracking and maneuver capability, local extraction and recycling, inspection robots and modular spares, compute and communications. The crucial question is never whether these requirements exist but whether the complex can reproduce the industries that satisfy them faster than the system depreciates.

This is the causal boundary of the expansion claim. Moving an Earth-manufactured factory or data center into orbit changes its environmental and transport terms but leaves its growth exogenous to the off-world system. Moving a reproducible production graph off Earth does two different things: closure makes growth endogenous, while location relaxes terrestrial biosphere, land, and political rate limits. Either property can exist without the other. “Free industrial expansion” is shorthand for their conjunction, never for costless matter, energy, control, or time.

Gravity is a process variable, not a system prerequisite. Industrial operations divide into at least three classes: processes that tolerate or benefit from microgravity; bulk operations for which a stable downward acceleration assists excavation, hopper flow, phase separation, drainage, and conventional machine layout; and processes that need a specified acceleration, hydrostatic head, or convection regime. Lunar gravity (approximately 0.165g0.165g) supplies the second class with a persistent “down,” although lower weight also reduces traction and changes granular flow. It cannot be declared sufficient for every casting, chemical, lubrication, thermal, or separation process without testing that process.

Where greater acceleration is needed, the relevant intervention is normally a rotating drum, centrifuge, or production module—not artificial gravity for the entire industrial ecology. For radius RR and angular speed ω\omega,

ac=ω2R. a_c=\omega^2R.

One terrestrial gg requires about 30 rpm at a one-metre radius or 9.5 rpm at ten metres. An ideal rotor consumes energy to spin up and stores E=12Iω2E=\tfrac12 I\omega^2; it does not require continuous power merely to remain in uniform rotation. The engineering cost lies in structural mass, containment, bearings or magnetic suspension, balance control, vibration, rotating power/data interfaces, spin losses, maintenance, and the inability to share one acceleration geometry across every process. Lunar gravity is therefore plausibly enough for many bulk operations, while local centrifugation is the fallback for gravity-sensitive steps. No credible single dollar estimate exists without a process list, throughput, rotor radius, and maintenance architecture. The current model has none of these variables; a Fourth Edition process-environment matrix must add them.

The complex need not be one self-contained factory: a distributed network of specialized nodes — lunar mines, orbital refineries, propellant depots, solar arrays, shipyards, robot factories, compute centers — whose combined input-output graph is closed. A caution on aggregation applies to every use of the word “space” in this document: LEO, high orbit, cislunar space, the lunar poles, Mars, and asteroids differ in radiation, thermal cycling, illumination, dust, gravity, resources, transport energy, and serviceability. Feasibility claims cannot be aggregated across them; the model of Part I.7 therefore uses three explicit reference architectures (LEO / lunar surface / Mars) rather than “space.”

I.4 Depreciation: the harsh term

dHdt=(r−δ)H \frac{\mathrm d H}{\mathrm d t} = (r-\delta)H

Every claim ultimately reduces to whether gross replication r exceeds depreciation δ. Terrestrial industry operates inside an ambient repair economy; space assets have none of it. Published measurements constrain a few component classes without bounding the depreciation of an autonomous industrial ecology. GEO solar arrays degrade 0.4–1.7%/yr [S19], overlapping a terrestrial PV median of 0.5–0.6%/yr at the low end and reaching several times it at the high end [S18] — one component class, silent about bearings, seals, lubricated mechanisms, batteries, machine tools, robots, radiators, dust-exposed joints, and optical metrology. Constellation figures are cohort-dependent: reported early-generation failure fractions near 3% fall below 0.5% for later generations, the underlying tracker separates early deorbit, uncontrolled decay, retirement, and disposal, and a fleet fraction without a cohort definition, observation interval, and satellite-years is not a hazard rate [S22]. Large-training-run figures near one interruption per two thousand accelerator-days mix permanent component failures with recoverable host, network, and software events, most automatically recovered: a cluster interruption frequency, not a component hazard rate. The space penalty is not faster wear — it is the five-to-fifteen-year no-repair design envelope, the internality of every repair chain, and discrete loss without salvage. Meanwhile the most advanced terrestrial capital — fab tools on 2–5-year schedules, accelerators retired within 3–6 years — is written off faster than a space solar array physically degrades, because obsolescence sets the terrestrial clock. Five quantities must be kept apart, since compressing them was an internal inconsistency of earlier revisions: physical degradation, functional failure, accounting depreciation, economic obsolescence, and frontier-equivalent capacity loss. The last two are not physical — an accelerator does not vanish because a better one exists — so obsolescence enters a growth equation only where H is measured in quality-adjusted or frontier-equivalent capacity, which is why the terrestrial paradox above is a statement about accounting and frontier equivalence rather than about matter. The corrected form, decomposed per environment,

δtotal=δwear+δcatastrophic+δobsolescence+δlogistics \delta_{\mathrm{total}} = \delta_{\mathrm{wear}} + \delta_{\mathrm{catastrophic}} + \delta_{\mathrm{obsolescence}} + \delta_{\mathrm{logistics}}

is what the model implements per sector and per location: space raises the catastrophic and logistics terms, Earth raises the obsolescence term, wear differs modestly. The environment drives δ upward through radiation, thermal cycling, micrometeoroids and debris, vacuum effects, regolith dust, and the absence of an ambient repair economy.

Collision-cascade boundary. Kessler risk is not an unusually large independent shock. It is positive feedback: collisions create orbiting fragments, those fragments raise the later collision rate, and a sufficiently dense region can keep generating debris after new deployment stops [S45]. The implemented LEO debris_event instead has a fixed 0.05 yr⁻¹ Poisson rate and removes a sampled 0.02–0.10 fraction from selected sectors; the loss creates no debris and the event rate does not depend on deployed capacity. The continuous catastrophic term is likewise an exogenous hazard range. These terms can represent ordinary impact exposure but cannot test the cascade threshold, avoidance saturation, shell-to-shell fragment transport, or recovery through decay and active removal. Accordingly, every LEO scale result in I.8 is conditional on a subcritical debris reproduction regime. The missing state is not assigned a silent proxy value: the queued model tracks active assets, intact derelicts, trackable and untrackable fragments, collision-generated yield, disposal and natural clearance by orbital region, with the expected hazardous descendants per object as the reported cascade metric. Lunar orbit does not remove the issue: breakup simulations find strongly orbit-dependent evolution, including long-lasting debris in some low lunar orbits and nonuniform lunar impact patterns [S46].

One environment-specific case partly cuts the other way, and it is narrower than earlier drafts of this argument claimed. For short-lived compute payloads in very low orbit, aligning useful life with accelerator-obsolescence cycles reduces the economic value of repair: hardware retired within a few years regardless costs less to be unable to fix. The qualifications are substantial. Natural decay depends on altitude, ballistic coefficient, and solar activity — small spacecraft near 400 km can decay within five years, but beyond 500 km five-year decay is not assured and some objects persist for decades. The leading published orbital-compute architecture in fact places its cluster near 650 km in dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit, chosen for illumination rather than disposal [S32], so the concession does not describe that design. Nor is disposal free: station-keeping, collision avoidance (large-constellation operators report on the order of a thousand automated avoidance manoeuvres per day [S27]), replacement launch, network spares, and residual capital loss all remain — and the same source that calls orbital repair impracticable proposes redundant provisioning instead, which is a cost, not its absence [S32]. The concession therefore covers some payload classes in very low orbit and does not generalize: mines, refineries, machine tools, and robots must last decades to close a loop. In this edition’s models it lands in Part II (compute payloads), while Part I’s industrial δ decomposition stands.

The clock-setting component. In any partially closed system, the component with the shortest replacement horizon that cannot yet be produced or stockpiled locally sets a hard clock on the entire complex. Closure priority follows failure rate × non-replaceability, not mass fraction. Viability requires:

Treplenish,j<Teffectivesurvival,jfor every critical component class j T_{\mathrm{replenish},j} < T_{\mathrm{effective\ survival},j} \qquad \text{for every critical component class } j

Effective survival includes installed redundancy, spare inventory, repair probability, and substitution — not time to first failure; the naive form ignored inventories, cannibalisation, and stockpiling, all of which the model carries explicitly. Part I.7 enforces the condition by construction, and Part I.8 identifies the clock-setting sector by computation.

I.5 Industrial closure: the decisive threshold

Self-sustaining (P ≥ D: replacing what is consumed and lost) and self-expanding (P > D: net additional capacity) are different milestones.

gH=P−DH g_H = \frac{P-D}{H}

P is productive capacity manufactured during the period, D is capacity lost or consumed, and g_H is the net growth rate of productive infrastructure H. Industrial closure measures how much of the critical input set is produced inside the network:

κ=critical inputs produced internallytotal critical inputs required \kappa = \frac{\text{critical inputs produced internally}} {\text{total critical inputs required}}

At low closure, nearly all equipment arrives from Earth. At intermediate closure, local industry supplies propellant, shielding, structural material, tanks, roads, landing pads, and simple machines. At high closure, the network also supplies motors, sensors, precision tools, computers, robots, spacecraft, factory machinery, high-purity chemicals, and semiconductor equipment. The progression is descriptive, not a scalar score: the identity and replaceability of the residual imports determine whether the system is actually autonomous.

The scalar form is vocabulary, not measure: a system supplying 99.9% of its mass locally has effectively zero autonomous closure if the residual 0.1% contains irreplaceable electronics. The proper object is a vector — κ = (κ_power, κ_mining, κ_refining, κ_robotics, κ_machine-tools, κ_electronics, κ_thermal, κ_transport) — with minimum cut sets: the smallest combinations of imported inputs whose loss collapses the network. The clock-setting component is the scalar shadow of this vector structure. The hardest closure problem is the deep technological stack — precision metrology, advanced electronics, semiconductor fabrication, optics, high-purity processing, specialized alloys, software verification, and the machines that make those machines (Part III.2 examines the first announced attempt on its terrestrial face).

Lineage. The 1980 NASA/ASEE summer study [S23] designed a proof-of-principle self-replicating lunar factory growing from a ~100-ton seed and introduced quantitative closure analysis; Freitas and Merkle catalogued the design space [S24]. The present thesis is that lineage plus three ingredients the 1980 study lacked: general-purpose AI for coordination and redesign; a reusable heavy-lift transport economy; mass-manufactured robotics.

The r and κ anchors, read honestly. Three quantities sometimes presented as converging near one year are not commensurable: solar energy payback (0.9–4.1 yr [S20]) measures energy self-financing only; the NASA ~1-year figure is a strawman replication target adopted for a proof-of-principle design, with component values its authors described as crude estimates; Gigafactory Shanghai’s ten months is project execution inside a mature host economy. The replication time of a full industrial ecosystem is unknown — and in this edition it appears only as a computed output distribution (Part I.8). For κ, the honest headline is a theory-versus-demonstration gap, stated as its authors did: the 90–96% figure is closure reported in earlier studies of specific nonreplicating space-production applications, cited in support of plausibility arguments, and the 1980 study explicitly placed a rigorous demonstration that its factory could produce every required part beyond its own scope [S23]. Against that sit gram-per-hour oxygen and ~20% single-output yields actually demonstrated [S21]; full-system closure has not been demonstrated at any scale. Stating the gap converts the indicators of Part III.5 from rhetoric into measurable distance.

The seed-factory transition. Earth stops transporting finished infrastructure and transports a compact industrial seed, but “seed” denotes four inseparable layers:

  1. Physical seed: power, extraction, refining, manufacturing, robots, compute, repair capability, laboratories, and initial spares.
  2. Digital seed: AI weights, firmware, controllers, compilers, CAD/CAM, simulators, optimization and verification tools, and locally available software dependencies.
  3. Industrial memory: process recipes, material and equipment models, calibration and maintenance procedures, complete build records, failure histories, intervention traces, and provenance.
  4. Experimental capacity: instrumentation, metrology, test stands, flexible machine tools, and the ability to run controlled experiments that produce missing knowledge.

The package is viable only if it grows its productive base before critical imported components fail. A credible design requires redundancy, recycling, buffers, and an explicit path for every nonlocal dependency. No finite seed can encode “all necessary industrial knowledge”; the operational criterion is a sufficient foundation plus the capacity to diagnose gaps, run experiments, validate new processes, and incorporate the results into industrial memory. The current model represents only part of the physical layer: spares are a shelf-life-limited stockpile state; nonlocal dependencies are import lines that can be cut; and the “explicit path” is an abstract sector requirement matrix. It does not yet size the seed or represent its digital, memory, experimental, commissioning, or knowledge-generation requirements.

I.6 Intelligence, coordination, and time

A sufficiently capable AI converts much of organizational coordination from human management into a computational service: scheduling machines, allocating energy, routing materials, diagnosing failures, redesigning components, balancing inventories, planning construction, and selecting the next bottleneck to expand.

Coordination burden = f(number of agents, complexity, uncertainty,
                        intelligence)

As intelligence and local autonomy improve, human management per unit of infrastructure can decline. The likely architecture is hierarchical rather than perfectly centralized: local controllers manage fast operations; facility-level agents coordinate production; higher-level planners allocate resources across the network.

An early terrestrial version is documented inside the same corporate ecosystem, though more weakly than some commentary claims. The confirmed systems provide real-time monitoring, production scheduling, quality management, and linked operational data at factory scale [S14–S17]. That is evidence that coordination functions traditionally mediated by human management are already becoming industrial data infrastructure. Claims that the systems replace management wholesale remain commentary-class and are not used here.

Coordination cannot reach zero. Machines must sense their environment; signals require propagation time; fabrication and motion take time; uncertain results require verification; and two projects cannot consume the same indivisible machine or stored resource simultaneously:

Cmin=Csensing+Clatency+Cverification+Ccontention C_{\min} = C_{\mathrm{sensing}} + C_{\mathrm{latency}} + C_{\mathrm{verification}} + C_{\mathrm{contention}}

The strongest claim is therefore that coordination approaches a physical information floor, not zero. Intelligence compresses planning and management overhead until observation, propagation, actuation, and verification dominate.

After high closure the central allocation question changes from “can the system obtain land, permits, workers, components?” to “how much productive throughput should be assigned, and when will output be complete?”

Hcreated(t)=∫0tthroughput⁡(τ)dτ H_{\mathrm{created}}(t) = \int_0^t \operatorname{throughput}(\tau)\,\mathrm d\tau

Scarcity becomes latency: outputs remain scarce because machines, energy, compute, transport capacity, and time have alternative uses, but the accounting unit shifts toward joules, kilograms processed, machine-hours, compute-hours, delta-v, and completion time. In the mature regime, growth is bottleneck-limited:

dHdt=(r(I,E,M)−δ)H \frac{\mathrm d H}{\mathrm d t} = \bigl(r(I,E,M)-\delta\bigr)H

When r exceeds δ, the idealized solution is exponential growth; real systems instead move through phases in which extraction, refining, power, thermal control, transport, precision manufacturing, compute, or maintenance is slowest. Intelligence accelerates growth by detecting and expanding that active bottleneck. The model below is an idealized, perfectly informed coordination benchmark, not a demonstration that an AI can observe, verify, and execute the allocation across a distributed factory. Within that benchmark, the co-binding set reported each step identifies the modeled active bottleneck.

I.7 The multisector model

The model that produces a real number needs, per critical sector, a capacity, a sector-specific depreciation, a requirement matrix, and import terms — with self-expansion defined as feasible positive net growth in every indispensable sector. Earlier editions specified it; this one builds and runs it [S28]. Its elements:

  • Ten sectors: power, mining, refining, robotics, machine tools, electronics, thermal, transport, and — as first-class service sectors — repair and recycling. Repair offsets the wear component of δ only, at an efficiency cost and a spares bill drawn from the requirement matrix; recycling converts retiring capacity into secondary mining/refining feed, capped by the scrap flow.
  • Requirement matrix A[i,j]: units of sector-i output consumed to build one unit of sector-j capacity. ASSUMPTION-class, existing only inside named assumption sets (conservative / central / optimistic).
  • Decomposed δ per environment (I.4’s four components, per sector), assembled from anchored ranges where anchors exist and declared ASSUMPTION entries elsewhere. Retirement does not become fully recyclable scrap: environment- and loss-specific recovery factors distinguish wear, catastrophic loss, obsolescence, and logistics loss, and repaired wear is subtracted from retirement. Each resolved value carries a provenance record into the generated reports.
  • The allocation step: a three-stage lexicographic optimization. Stage 1 maximizes worst-case sector growth g over builds, wear repair, recycling, and stockpile drawdown. Stage 2 holds g at its optimum and minimizes normalized stock draw. Stage 3 holds both objectives and solves a strictly convex, normalized quadratic program over actions. This final tie-breaker makes the state transition unique instead of accepting an arbitrary LP vertex.
  • Vitamin-part stockpiles with shelf life (electronics-class parts decay in storage), per-sector import cutoffs, Mars synodic pulse deliveries, and Poisson common-cause shocks hitting correlated sector groups (dust storms, solar storms, common design faults).
  • Three reference architectures: LEO (no ore body — feed imported or recycled from on-orbit mass), lunar surface (regolith feed, abrasive dust, no magnetosphere), Mars (deepest logistics penalty, ~26-month synodic resupply pulses, dust storms).

The vitamin gap. One structural parameter dominates the scoped scalar-anchor screen in I.8: the fraction of the electronics sector’s output mix producible off-world (sampled 0.10–0.30; ASSUMPTION with a documented bounding argument — advanced-node logic and memory are not locally closable in this scenario class; boards, passives, and power electronics plausibly are). No published measurement exists. Among the quantities currently parameterized, anchoring it is the highest-value observation this program could acquire; that ranking does not cover omitted structural uncertainties.

The new model asks whether a declared physical seed can supply the material, service, autonomy, and commissioning requirements of another operating cell. It expands the graph to 18 sectors — separating interconnects/boards, passives/power, sensors/control, packaging/test, mature logic, advanced logic/memory, and process equipment/metrology — and 17 material categories. Allocation-policy robustness. The results above could in principle be an artifact of the myopic one-step maximin allocator — a forward-looking operator might accept lower current worst-case growth to build future capacity. Because the dynamics and balances are linear, the finite-horizon LP is solved exactly — though receding-horizon control remains an approximation to full-horizon optimization (truncation and terminal-value effects persist; an investment paying off after year six is invisible to a six-year window). On paired draws (identical parameter samples per policy, shocks off), a six-year receding-horizon maximin reproduces the myopic policy essentially exactly — 16/30 vs 16/30 sampled runs expanding on the lunar surface, 11/30 vs 11/30 in LEO, with matching co-binding shares (now computed with the same finite 1% response and co-binding definition as the baseline, so this comparison tests the claim it appears to test — an r3.5 fix; the lunar count itself swung from 22/30 to 25/30 to 16/30 across regenerations at this small paired n — the policy equality is the stable finding, the level is not) — because the maximin criterion already directs each step’s investment at the worst-off sector, which is the future bottleneck. A third policy, maximizing aggregate growth subject to no sector shrinking, collapses (0/30 in both architectures): worst-case allocation is load-bearing, not incidental. Bottleneck-first heuristics are subsumed by the maximin construction itself. The defensible conclusion is correspondingly scoped: under paired, shock-free draws for the tested LEO and lunar central cases, a six-year receding horizon did not materially change the outcomes; global allocation-policy robustness (longer horizons, perfect foresight, robust control under shocks) is not established — and the retooling analysis of Part IV exhibits one regime where allocation family genuinely matters. I.9 Development path, annotated

  1. Terrestrial autonomous-closure rehearsal. Before launch, operate an integrated seed factory on Earth using only the material forms, energy intermittency, communications constraints, spares allowance, and production methods intended for the lunar deployment. Exclude routine human entry; route every human intervention through a logged maintenance interface; inject equipment faults and communication outages; and require the system to manufacture, assemble, calibrate, and commission a second production cell. This stage does not reproduce lunar gravity, vacuum, radiation, or abrasive dust, so environmental qualification remains separate. It does test whether the industrial graph and its encoded operating knowledge are autonomous enough to export. Report intervention hours per machine-hour, imported mass per unit of added local capacity, critical bill-of-material lines producible, autonomous recovery fraction, time to a commissioned second cell, and every remaining single-point dependency.

  2. Earth-dependent operations. Earth supplies vehicles, energy systems, consumables, spares, electronics, and nearly all machinery; space assets perform narrow missions.

  3. Local-resource supplementation. Local water, oxygen, shielding, construction feedstock, and selected propellant inputs reduce imported mass without yet closing the industrial loop.

  4. Heavy-industry closure. The network produces bulk metals, glass, ceramics, structural parts, tanks, simple mechanisms, roads, pads, and large power structures.

  5. Advanced-manufacturing closure. It adds motors, actuators, sensors, precision tools, robots, spacecraft subsystems, computers, and factory machinery. Terafab is best read as a terrestrial rehearsal of this stage (Part III.2): advanced-manufacturing integration practiced on Earth before any attempt to export it.

  6. Self-sustenance. Internal production replaces depreciation and failure across every mission-critical category; Earth supply becomes optional rather than load-bearing.

  7. Self-expansion. The system builds more mines, refineries, factories, power systems, robots, transports, and compute than replacement requires.

  8. Intelligence-industry recursion. Additional compute improves intelligence; improved intelligence raises industrial replication efficiency; higher output creates still more compute.

    I increases → replication efficiency increases → infrastructure increases → compute increases → I increases

The bridge economy maps onto the ladder: terrestrial products carry the early stages; launch and connectivity carry Stages 2–4; orbital compute is a candidate Stage 4–5 tread; and in-space industrial services carry Stages 5–6. The model adds stage warnings. The Stage 4→5 transition is where the vitamin gap binds — no amount of Stage 3 bulk closure substitutes for an electronics tier. Stage 5 is location-dependent: LEO requires a sustained capture-and-recovery feed loop; under the present 5% capture assumption, recycling is valuable but not knockout-indispensable at central values. Mars requires electronics stockpiles engineered to synodic shelf life or a local electronics tier. The lunar surface has the fastest conditional balanced-doubling pathway when electronics closes sufficiently; LEO has the more durable central endpoint under its continuing-capture assumption.

Location sequence and the lunar hybrid. The development ladder is not a ladder of orbital altitudes. In particular, Sun-synchronous orbit is a planet-centered orbit whose plane precesses to preserve approximately the same local solar time [S49]. The familiar dawn-dusk SSO is still LEO—the 650 km orbital-compute design assessed in Part II is an example—not the destination after a lunar surface base.

For closure, the more coherent topology is:

  1. LEO as a bridge, not the terminal reservoir. Communications, observation, early compute, assembly demonstrations, and transport can earn revenue and test systems, but bulk growth remains exposed to finite imported/captured feed and density-dependent collision risk.
  2. Lunar surface as the material backbone. Extraction, bulk refining, construction, durable storage, shielding, and long-life machinery use a physical foundation and local regolith. South-polar planning seeks the conjunction of long illumination intervals and access to shadowed volatile deposits, while leaving reserve size and extraction economics unresolved; dust and landing ejecta remain major surface penalties [S47].
  3. Sparse lunar/cislunar orbit as the service layer. Communications, navigation, observation, reusable transports, depots, assembly, and selected compute occupy orbit only where zero-g or illumination benefits repay shielding, stationkeeping, and service costs. NRHO is a candidate gateway geometry designed for eclipse avoidance and low stationkeeping, not a substitute ore body or a passive maintenance-free base [S48].
  4. Distributed heliocentric expansion after lunar export closure. Productive nodes then spread across separated Sun-orbiting trajectories and resource paths—potentially including near-Earth objects—rather than reproducing one dense shell. The point is diversity of orbital elements and feedstocks, not one privileged “solar orbit.” Moving inward raises solar flux but also heat-rejection, radiation, communications, and materials constraints, so no optimal solar radius is claimed.

Material-source sequence. The Moon is not chemically equivalent to Earth, but neither is it short of bulk machine-building elements. Its crust is dominated by oxygen, silicon, magnesium, iron, calcium, and aluminum, with smaller titanium-bearing and other phases; composition varies strongly between highlands and mare terrains [S51]. Those elements support oxygen, glass and ceramics, aluminum, iron-bearing alloys, silicon feed, shielding, structures, and many bulk machine parts, subject to extraction energy, purification, alloying, yield, and process-equipment constraints. The early system can therefore treat the lunar surface as a bulk-material backbone while importing low-mass, high-criticality material vitamins from Earth: advanced chips, dopants, catalysts, specialty chemicals, bearings, sensors, precision tools, metrology, and any alloying elements whose local recovery is not yet viable.

An order-of-magnitude inventory check prevents “no iron” from becoming a false premise. At an average regolith bulk density near 1.5 t/m³ [S52], one square kilometre excavated to one metre contains about 1.5 million tonnes of regolith. Applying the approximately 3 wt% iron reported for lunar highlands material [S53] gives roughly 45,000 tonnes of iron in place. This is an illustrative inventory, not an ore reserve: it says nothing about site homogeneity, mineral form, excavation losses, energy, recovery yield, environmental impact, economics, or how much is practically extractable. It does show why asteroid mining is not a prerequisite for initial lunar iron.

The harder question is diversity. Lunar carbon and nitrogen occur at low concentrations in returned fines, while polar water evidence remains spatially heterogeneous and does not yet provide a bankable reserve estimate [S54, S55]. A robot-only ecology avoids agricultural and biosphere-scale demand, but it still uses volatiles and specialty elements for propellant, reduction chemistry, cooling, lubrication, polymers, process gases, electronics, and high-performance alloys. Carbonaceous asteroids and differentiated metal-rich bodies can eventually add water, carbon-bearing material, nickel, cobalt, and other feedstocks; returned Bennu material confirms carbon-rich organics and a history of water-related mineral chemistry in at least one carbonaceous body [S56]. Asteroids are therefore modeled as a follow-on route to diversity and supply sovereignty, not invoked as the first source of every machine metal.

Current demonstrations make individual conversion paths more concrete without closing the system. NASA describes integrated autonomous processing of regolith simulant into silicon solar cells, aluminum wire, oxygen, iron, and slag, alongside vacuum tests of molten-regolith electrolysis [S57]. These are meaningful subsystem demonstrations; they are not a lunar mine, an operational supply chain, a factory reproducing its own equipment, or an audited second production cell.

This ranks functions, not prestige destinations. Surface and orbit are complements: orbit is the transfer and service network; the surface supplies bulk matter and shielding. The inherited ten-sector and Part II models cannot compare that hybrid directly: they isolate lunar-surface capacity, cislunar compute, and Earth-supplied lunar compute, and they aggregate elements into industries. The Fourth layer adds element bills of material, source yields, Earth–Moon–orbit–asteroid arcs, and aggregate endogenous debris states. It therefore computes an explicit hybrid scenario, but its recipes, yields, transfer capacities, composite asteroid source, and collision kernels are declared assumptions. It does not establish lunar material sufficiency, a recoverable reserve, a launch date for asteroid supply, or an orbital safety case.

I.10 Binding bottlenecks and failure modes

With model annotations: lithography and fabrication equipment (the named residual chokepoint one layer below foundries — and the deepest single closure problem in this framework); reliability and repair (the model makes mean-time-to-repair and spares closure first-class: repair is an explicit sector with an efficiency and a spares bill); thermal management (radiators are launched mass — Part II’s radiator axis); energy storage and continuity (the lunar night storage mass term; Mars dust-storm shocks); resource quality (LEO has no ore body, so the sustained capture-and-recovery feed loop binds even though the separately modeled recycling sector is not knockout-indispensable at central values); verification and control (Part III.4); political and legal environment; economic survival of the transition (Part II.1); concentrated coordination (single points of failure a distributed competitor lacks); paradigm-shift stranding (Part IV.3 — exposure concentrated in the fabrication layer; the fleet self-insures through its own 3–6-year depreciation).

Part II — Comparative Economics

II.1 The bridge economy

Each stage of the recursion must be independently profitable or deliberately financed: a staircase of self-supporting steps, not a single leap to closure. The plausible treads, in order: terrestrial products of the same machinery (vehicles, storage, robots — the ground floor pays rent and turns the autonomous seed into an audited industrial experiment); launch and connectivity (Starlink revenue and launch services fund transport); orbital compute services — a candidate tread, now priced rather than merely doubted (II.2–II.4); and in-space industrial services at higher closure. The test of the staircase: at every stage, revenue plus strategic financing must exceed the burn required to reach the next stage before the clock-setting components of Part I.4 expire — and Part I.8 makes the clock concrete: the electronics stockpile shelf life against the import ramp.

Stage 0 has a second economic role. Building the candidate lunar industries first on Earth captures process knowledge, failure data, tool interfaces, simulation residuals, and commissioning procedures while the repair economy is still accessible. Selling terrestrial output can finance that learning, but the decisive deliverable is the measured ability to build and commission a second cell under lunar-palette and no-routine-entry constraints. A factory that is profitable yet requires tacit human rescue has not validated the exported loop.

II.2 The crossover model

Unit: dollars per frontier-equivalent kilowatt-year of delivered IT load — defined as one kilowatt-year of IT electrical load powering accelerators of frontier-generation efficiency, delivered at the case’s utilization; older-node capacity is converted by its J/FLOP penalty, so a frontier-equivalent kilowatt-year buys the same computational output regardless of which hardware generation supplies it. Three reference cases: a LEO inference service; high-orbit/cislunar batch compute; and Earth-supplied lunar-surface compute, with local manufacture entering only through the closure sweep. Mass and power budgets resolve from anchored ranges — launch price sheets (company_statement), flexible-array specific power and deployable-radiator specific mass (technical_report), accelerator system cost and lives (reporting), DC capex, PUE, WACC (reporting), qualification multiplier and utilization (ASSUMPTION, evidence-tempered) — each with a source class and per-run provenance. The space cost is affine in launch $/kg, so break-even launch prices are exact. Chip efficiency is a first-class parameter: off Earth, every watt not computed must be radiated, and radiator, PV, storage, and structure are launched mass, so a FLOPS/W advantage scales the entire power side per unit of delivered compute — efficiency and launch price enter the crossover multiplicatively, with a quantitative refinement below. This makes the popular framing of orbital compute as a launch-price race incomplete and supplies a thermodynamic rationale for internalizing chip design, not merely fabrication or supply security: the most efficient accelerator can tolerate a higher launch price because it carries less power and heat-rejection mass per useful computation.

Two lifecycle architectures are reported separately:

  • Integrated disposable satellite: compute, PV, radiator, storage, structure, and original launch spend retire together.
  • Serviceable/reusable platform: the compute payload refreshes on its hardware life while the power/thermal/structure platform and its original launch spend amortize over a longer infrastructure life. This is an explicit scenario assumption; modularity alone does not demonstrate serviceability.

Post-IPO architecture calibration. The technical literature available by late July 2026 does not supply one new “correct” mass-to-power ratio. It separates three architectures that should not be blended into one Monte Carlo distribution. Turyshev’s independent 1 MW high-sunlight closure allocates 29.4 kg/kW to PV, storage, and radiators; fixed spacecraft functions raise the total to 34–59 kg/kW [S39]. This independently brackets the model’s 46 kg/kW central generic LEO case and supports its order-of-magnitude placement, although the model’s full component-corner envelope (about 25–91 kg/kW) is wider. AI1’s derived 14.3–17.9 kg/kW sits below that conventional envelope and remains a separate company-specification case. Below both are research architectures: an integrated solar-compute-radiator panel concept at about 8.9 kg/kW and a tethered inference architecture at about 5 kg/kW [S40]. Those figures are legitimate lower-bound scenarios, but depend respectively on kilometre-scale integrated arrays, unqualified vapor chambers, thin tandem PV and custom ASICs, or on long tethers, distributed deployment, shielding, station-keeping, and micrometeoroid tolerance. They are pre-prototype architecture claims, not evidence that the generic spacecraft prior should move to 5–10 kg/kW.

Thermal closure is a system, not a radiator-panel coefficient. NASA’s flight and mission studies distinguish 2.75 kg/m² ISS radiator panels from 8.8 kg/m² after fluid loops and deployment are included; a JIMO study estimated 5 kg/m² for the complete system. At 353 K radiator temperature, 0.86 emissivity, and a 173 K environmental sink, the cited sizing is about 1.4 m²/kW, implying roughly 7–12 kg/kW for the complete heat-rejection system [S43]. This supports the middle and upper part of the model’s 3–12 kg/kW range, but shows that its low end represents advanced emitters, not a demonstrated full stack of cold plates, pumps, transport lines, deployment, and controls.

AI1 makes the ambiguity visible. Its published 110 m² radiator for 120 kW average compute is 0.92 m²/kW of quoted area [S38]. If 110 m² is physical footprint and both faces emit, the mean flux is about 545 W/m²; a simple Stefan–Boltzmann balance using NASA’s emissivity and environmental temperature gives an ideal radiator surface near 332 K (59 °C). If 110 m² instead denotes total emitting area, the load is about 1,090 W/m² and the ideal surface approaches 391 K (118 °C). Both calculations omit solar and Earth view, degradation, nonuniformity, pumping power, and the chip-to-coolant-to-radiator temperature drop. The disclosure is therefore not thermodynamically impossible, but its area convention, coolant temperature, and full thermal-system mass are crossover-critical measurements.

The company’s own scale target exposes the feasibility burden. At 100 GW deployed annually and 100 kW/t, the flow is one million tonnes/year: 10,000 full 100-t V3 launches (27.4/day), or 5,000 full 200-t V4 launches (13.7/day), and roughly 833,000 AI1-sized 120-kW-average spacecraft. Even at $185/kg, launch cost alone is $185 billion/year before spacecraft manufacture. At the later AI1 page’s disclosed 70 kW/t, the same flow rises to 1.43 million tonnes and 14,286 full V3 launches/year. The current design is therefore 30% below the older 100 kW/t assumption; reaching it requires 43% more kW/t. Full rapid reuse, daily turnaround at unprecedented cadence, upper-stage recovery, fleet size, range capacity, and manufacturing are load-bearing. So are seven-year whole-system reliability and 95% compute utilization, neither published for AI1. Spectrum and regulatory approvals, collision avoidance and debris, replenishment, networking, insurance, program NRE, and landing/turnaround infrastructure remain excluded, while terrestrial energy, chips, cooling, permitting, and construction can also improve. The prospectus itself identifies approvals and spectrum as dependencies and warns that better terrestrial energy could reduce the orbital advantage [S37].

The post-IPO review sharpens the lifetime interpretation. The model’s 10–25-year infrastructure life is defensible only if the power and thermal platform survives multiple accelerator generations and the compute payload can actually be exchanged, serviced, or economically separated. For an integrated, non-serviceable LEO vehicle, the relevant infrastructure life is instead close to the spacecraft or compute life—typically about five years in the studies reviewed here [S39–S42]. The serviceable-platform and integrated-disposable results above now expose both lifecycle readings. Shortening every component to five years is appropriate for an integrated vehicle, but too pessimistic once modular payload replacement is demonstrated. Platform reuse is a named architecture gate, not an innocuous amortization choice.

A negative break-even means fixed, non-launch costs alone exceed the Earth cost: at those draws no launch price, even zero, closes the gap. The structural finding: the crossover is gated by the space-qualification premium, equipment life, and utilization — not launch price alone. The generic-LEO crossover surface (launch $/kg × life × qualification × radiator × closure × chip efficiency) shows no ratio-below-1 region at published prices on any single axis, and near-parity only in the defined Starship aspiration cases ($185/kg formal; $100/kg aggressive; $30–50/kg extreme) combined with favorable values on at least two other axes. AI1’s disclosed aggregate mass is a multi-parameter architecture constraint rather than a single-axis point on that generic surface; it produces the conditional near-published break-even above while leaving no competitive draw in either lifecycle ensemble. At the central LEO architecture’s approximately 46 kg/kW, those prices correspond to initial launch expenditures of about $8,510, $4,600, $2,300, and $1,380 per installed IT kW, respectively, before annualization. They largely remove the current launch bill, but a negative break-even still means they cannot overcome the fixed qualification/life/utilization gap on their own. Lunar closure is the weakest lever: chips are never closed, so even 95% closure of non-chip mass leaves the lunar case two orders of magnitude from parity at published prices.

The mass-multiplier refinement: at anchored values a 2× FLOPS/W advantage buys only ~40% of the benefit of halving launch price (ratio 4.0 vs 3.2 at $3,000/kg, both from 4.5), because the hardware term — untouched by efficiency — dominates both mass and capex. “The compute tax outweighs the launch tax” [S34] becomes true only after the qualification premium and hardware $/kW fall; near-term, both taxes are subordinate to qualification and life.

II.4 External claim and independent-study checks

The two public crossover claims are recorded as comparison targets (never inputs), and both reproduce inside the model under identifiable conditions — which converts a public disagreement into a localized, testable one. The post-IPO review of independent studies below then checks the model rather than merely restating corporate claims. One preliminary correction of the record, because this document earlier mis-stated it: the Suncatcher design study does not claim system-level parity. Its cost section states twice that it is not a full economic analysis and not a comprehensive economic feasibility study, and its comparison sets the amortised launch component alone (≈$810/kW-yr for a Starlink-v2-class mass-to-power ratio at $200/kg; $810–7,500 across satellite classes) beside terrestrial data-centre electricity spend (≈$570–3,000/kW-yr), explicitly excluding infrastructure, building, and chip costs [S32]. That is a launch-cost feasibility marker — the finding that launch need not remain independently prohibitive — not a parity claim, and it is consistent with this model, which reproduces launch as a minor term. The crossover claim this model contradicts is the stronger reading in circulation [S34], not the study’s own.

  • The Google/Planet study (reported: crossover at $200–300/kg [S34]). At $250/kg with central anchors: space/Earth = 2.04 — no crossover. Under the favorable corner within anchored ranges (qualification ×1.2, 7-year life, 0.95 utilization, 3 kg/kW radiators): 0.88 — the claim reproduced. The dispute is three numbers, none of them launch price.
  • The Suncatcher design study [S32]: an 81-satellite dawn-dusk sun-synchronous cluster near 650 km, terabit-class optical links (an 800 Gbps unidirectional bench demonstration against a stated ~10 Tbps per-link requirement), and a learning-curve projection of ≲$200/kg by the mid-2030s — itself conditional on roughly 370,000 tonnes of additional cumulative mass launched, on the order of 1,800 Starship-class flights averaging ~180 per year, which the authors call ambitious. Its engineering results should be read precisely (arXiv v2, 17 June 2026): HBM was the most TID-sensitive subsystem (irregularities from ~2 krad(Si) against a ~750 rad(Si) shielded five-year requirement, no hard total-dose failures to 15 krad(Si)), while core logic and on-chip SRAM were the most SEE-sensitive; the reported inference error probability is roughly one per three million inferences under the stated assumptions, with the effect on training explicitly reserved. At ~150 rad(Si)/yr, a seven-year dose (~1.05 krad) sits below the ~2 krad irregularity onset — so the radiation results support five-year TID survivability with margin and do not obviously contradict seven-year TID survival. What they do not establish is seven-year system reliability: SEE-induced corruption, host interruptions, training sensitivity, thermal management, and whole-spacecraft failure remain unresolved, with orbital repair called impracticable and redundant provisioning proposed instead — a cost, not an absence of one. The COTS practice supporting a low qualification premium (unmodified accelerators flying on imaging satellites [S34]) demonstrates cheapness, not five-year reliability.

The independent checks localize four additional gates:

  • Conventional mass and economic closure. Turyshev closes PV, eclipse reserve, thermal rejection, fixed spacecraft functions, sustained communication, utilization, and finite life in a single framework [S39]. Its 34–59 kg/kW total independently brackets and supports the generic central mass at the order-of-magnitude level. Its more important result is the combined launch-plus-build ceiling: $250–1,000/kg at 40 kg/kW before communications and lifecycle penalties. Every additional $1,000/kW of communication or operations cost reduces that allowance by $25/kg. Current public launch prices consume more than the entire allowance before a spacecraft is built.
  • Low-mass architectures are conditional, not contradictory. The integrated-panel and tether studies reach 8.9 and roughly 5 kg/kW respectively [S40], demonstrating that mass is an architecture variable rather than a universal constant. Both target inference and both exchange conventional subsystem maturity for deployment, structural, thermal, and custom-silicon risk. They justify explicit stretch scenarios; they do not justify assigning conventional spacecraft a five-kilogram prior.
  • Inference and training are different products. Van Berkel compares a terrestrial 8,000-accelerator Clos fabric with orbital meshes: about 28,800 TB/s of terrestrial bisection bandwidth versus approximately 2.25 TB/s for a two-dimensional and 10 TB/s for a three-dimensional orbital mesh at 100 Gbps links, with network latency 150–450 times higher [S41]. Even much faster optical links do not remove multi-hop diameter and collective-communication latency. The appropriate early comparison is therefore per-satellite or loosely coupled inference, preprocessing, and communication-integrated edge work—not frontier distributed training. Training should receive a network-performance model, not an ordinary utilization scalar.
  • Small space-native nodes already have a different cost structure. The peer-reviewed HPCA microdatacenter study finds power and thermal systems dominate bespoke small-satellite TCO, while compute hardware can be less than one percent; energy-efficiency improvements can reduce TCO by about two-thirds and are up to 2.5 times more valuable in space [S42]. This is encouraging for 0.5–10 kW Earth-observation preprocessing, where data originates in orbit and downlink is avoided. It is not evidence that a megawatt terrestrial-substitution service has the same economics.

The broader policy cross-check reaches the same conditional result by a different route. A University of Virginia assessment reports a simplified five-year 1 GW case at $135.6 billion in orbit versus $16.6 billion on Earth under present-style assumptions; near-parity appears only when launch falls to $30/kg, satellite hardware to $10/W, specific power rises to 100 W/kg, sunlight availability to 80%, and degradation and GPU failure both fall to 1% [S44]. Its model omits GPU cost, financing, insurance, logistics, and downtime and should not be treated as a precise forecast. Its contribution is the joint-condition result: parity emerges from several simultaneous improvements, not from launch price alone.

Consequence for the staircase, stated within the model’s service definition: the modeled orbital architectures are not cost-competitive as substitutes for terrestrial frontier compute at the stated assumptions. That conclusion now has a workload boundary: it applies directly to per-node inference in the cost model, while the new network evidence makes frontier distributed training less favorable than the scalar model shows. It does not price differentiated orbital products (in-orbit sensor processing, sovereign or military demand, resilience niches), which could be profitable while losing the commodity comparison. What follows for the staircase is correspondingly bounded: commodity-scale deployment ahead of the enabling corner is strategic positioning or learning investment rather than substitute-compute profit — an observation Part III converts into a discriminating test.

II.5 Competition and the takeoff-speed assumption

Explicit assumption. This thesis is decision-relevant chiefly under a slow-to-medium AI takeoff. Under a fast software takeoff, incumbent compute and algorithms decide outcomes before any physical loop closes; the space complex becomes something the resulting intelligence builds afterward, not the path by which it arises. The prominent published bet on the fast branch [S35] argues automated AI research compresses AGI-to-superintelligence into roughly a year; this document’s program is the other branch, and the disagreement operates on priors, not on any evidence item below.

The system is not alone. China is the closest existing implementation of the terrestrial stages at national scale (solar, batteries, robotics, rare-earth refining, trailing-edge fabrication) lacking, for now, a reusable heavy-lift economy. Hyperscalers close the same inputs with capital rather than ownership — power agreements, custom silicon, supply contracts — which works until aggregate demand outruns what markets can scale. State programs hold ISRU roadmaps but iterate slowly.

Two corrections to the launch-chokepoint assumption, both cutting against a durable launch monopoly: payload cost-performance improved by two to three orders of magnitude over the decade against a smaller multiple for launch price [S34] — a crossover model treating launch as the primary axis mis-weights its own sensitivity analysis (borne out in II.3, where the crossover is not launch-gated); and chemical rockets are a local optimum — mass-manufactured vehicles, electromagnetic or kinetic launch, and mass drivers from a low-gravity surface (named in the fabrication initiative’s own material [S29]) would reset the launch axis rather than move along it.

The capital wall. Expansion purchased through markets pays stacked scarcity rents; when demand outruns supply, prices are set by scarcity. Properly qualified: markets are not static — the right formulation is a race between demand growth and induced supply with multi-year capacity lags as the binding term, and whether internal replication beats inducing market expansion is a rate comparison (which is what the responsive counterfactual of II.2 implements on the power axis). A self-replicating base escapes mandatory dependence on external Earth markets — not economic allocation, opportunity cost, or internal pricing, which persist in any multi-firm or internally-priced closed ecology: expansion re-denominates from currency into materials, energy, and time. Two caveats: the entry ticket is still paid in dollars — the first self-replicating unit is bought, not grown, which is what the bridge economy exists to finance — and the logic predicts convergence, not uniqueness: every serious actor eventually verticalizes or plateaus; the durable advantage is the shortest hardware doubling time once everyone is. Historical reference classes calibrate “unprecedented”: Manhattan and Apollo peaked near 0.4% of GDP; British railway investment ran ~40% of GDP over a decade; wartime borrowing far exceeded either [S35]. Trillion-scale buildout is extreme but inside the envelope — the capital wall yields to expected returns; what does not yield is the dependency structure of Part I.8.

Integration versus modularity (the Coasean resolution). Markets scaled semiconductors a billionfold through disintegration; why would this time differ? A firm’s boundary sits where internal coordination costs exceed market transaction costs [S25]. AI attacks the internal term (Part I.6) — but it attacks the external one too, improving supplier discovery, specification, contracting, monitoring, quality assurance, pricing, logistics, and modular interface design. The correct conditional is therefore that AI moves the efficient boundary toward integration only if it reduces internal coordination costs faster than it reduces interfirm transaction costs, an empirical question this document does not settle; what does survive is that the market interface retains an irreducible capital requirement efficiency gains do not remove. The defensible conclusion is bounded: AI may move the efficient boundary toward integration — not that ownership integration outperforms modular ecosystems in general. Three qualifications are central: markets also supply experimentation, selection, redundancy, and price signals, which an integrated complex must replicate internally or decay into soft-budget pathologies; technical closure (the network can produce its inputs) must not be conflated with contractual or ownership closure — this thesis requires only the first; and the in-domain integration success story confounds integration with reusability, cadence, and incentives [S1].

The operating wall. Physics-priced inputs (energy, materials, machine-hours) fall with scale; society-priced inputs (land, permits, interconnection, litigation, goodwill) rise with it — on Earth, closure has a structural ceiling because land, law, and neighbors cannot be internalized. Space shrinks and relocates the society-priced term rather than abolishing it, with one favorable property: permission attaches to the gateway (launches, spectrum) rather than the complex, so the millionth robot needs no new municipal permit. The hard physical version: Earth has a planetary waste-heat budget; space offers radiator area not bounded by a planetary surface (though still bounded by matter, transport, geometry, and maintenance — Part IV) — though not free rejection. Two external observations bracket how hard the wall binds now: data-center siting is already politically contested over electricity, water, and land — the society-priced curve observed from inside the industry [S34] — while the AGI-buildout literature meets the same wall with natural gas and deregulation at a firm-power floor below the nuclear PPA, never mentioning orbit [S35]. The wall binds through politics more than physics or long-run capital — though near-term hardware (turbines, transformers, cooling, construction capacity) can also bind — and the difference between branches is a parameter in this edition’s model, not a mood. And the sequence remains brutal: before high closure, space is the most expensive place to operate — every kilogram of spares travels at full launch freight while depreciation runs at its worst (II.3’s ~4–9×) — so the declining-cost regime switches on only after κ is high, which is why the bridge economy and the clock-setting components decide who survives to reach it.

The threshold-model argument. If RSI capability is a threshold — a base model good enough to run verified improvement loops — a smarter starting model confers a head start that the loop erodes; the durable variable becomes who runs more loops, which is hardware. Mid-2026 evidence supports continued benchmark compression between open and proprietary models (open-weight releases benchmarking near the frontier [S12]); it does not establish commoditization at an autonomous-R&D threshold, so hardware dominance is the small-α branch of the model rather than the established trajectory. If RSI-grade capability does arrive in open weights, model advantage weakens sharply without evaporating — post-training, proprietary data, evaluation systems, inference infrastructure, tool integration, operational reliability, distribution, and the capacity to run physical experiments all persist as differentiators — and to that extent the contest shifts onto the physical loop, the strongest form of the verticalization thesis. The counterpoint: the argument holds only if capability is threshold-like rather than continuously compounding (L_RSI ∝ Aα·Cβ·V^γ, with α unknown); benchmark convergence does not establish that autonomous R&D capability is threshold-like. And the structural counterweight: models converge because they train on the same public data, so residual differentiation concentrates exactly in proprietary physical-process data (Part I.2) — if general capability commoditizes, the model contest narrows to the domains where training data is generated by owned industry, folding the model race back into the physical loop.

What was announced: a Tesla–SpaceX–xAI chip fabrication initiative (March 2026), joined by Intel in April; declared scope from chip design through lithography operation, fabrication, memory, packaging, and test; reported $20B initial plant with a filing-based ceiling near $119B; 2-nm target; mass production stated for 2028 [S6–S10]. The implementation detail matters. Reporting describes a roughly $3B research fab producing a few thousand wafers per month before any scaled phase, with ambitions moving from about 100,000 wafer starts per month toward one million [S6–S10]. The competence question needs stating precisely, since earlier drafts got it wrong: Tesla, SpaceX, and xAI have no advanced-fab operating record, but Intel plainly does, and the reported process plan is Intel 14A — so the open question is whether that experience transfers into this organisational structure and what obligations Intel has actually accepted, not whether anyone involved has run a fab. Two further corrections: the sites are distinct — a smaller Tesla research fab at the Giga Texas campus and a proposed SpaceX-led scaled facility in Grimes County, whose county tax and economic-development agreements are now recorded as fully executed, strengthening site-level commitment without constituting a definitive construction, funding, or operating agreement [S6–S10] — and scale comparisons used in earlier drafts are withdrawn: wafer-start counts cannot be set against a foundry’s global output without controlling for wafer diameter, node, product mix, yield, and effective die output, and “one terawatt per year” is a claimed annual production rate of hardware whose aggregate nameplate power draw would total a terawatt, a manufacturing-throughput figure not commensurable with national electricity consumption. The lithography question: for terrestrial operation, purchased lithography is an acceptable permanent dependency; for high space closure it must eventually be reproduced, substituted (older nodes at volume; nanoimprint; e-beam), or accepted as designed permanent import — the deepest single closure problem in this framework (and precisely where the Part I vitamin gap lives). This edition adds the bottleneck convergence: the AGI-buildout literature identifies advanced packaging and HBM memory as the near-term chip constraints [S35], and HBM is what the Suncatcher radiation testing found most dose-sensitive [S32] — the same component class is the terrestrial scaling bottleneck and the space reliability weak point.

Terafab-specific observables. The milestones that would change this assessment are: the first research-fab wafer and disclosed yield at any node; visible order flow from lithography, deposition, etch, and metrology suppliers; evidence that memory and advanced packaging are actually produced in-house rather than outsourced; radiation-qualified D3 silicon with disclosed orbital-compute unit economics; and definitive agreements that commit capital and counterparties. Industry skepticism is therefore a testable forecast, not a rhetorical counterweight [S10].

III.4 Control and governance of a self-expanding system

A self-expanding autonomous industrial network is, formally, a governed macroscale von Neumann replicator; the property that makes it valuable — compounding physical replication — makes its failure modes compound too. Verification must scale with expansion (V is a safety term); throttle authority must be explicit (rate limits, hardware-level authorization of replication steps, clarity about who holds them); the legal frame is unsettled and first movers set precedent.

The framework resolves its own tension — celebrating escape from society-priced costs while requiring retained constraint — through the gateway: in space, permission attaches to launches and spectrum, which is where governance holds enforceable leverage. The leverage variable is dependency — but not proportional to (1 − κ), since this document has already established that scalar closure is invalid and a single low-mass import can be a minimum cut conferring disproportionate leverage. The governing quantity is G_dependency = f(criticality, substitutability, stockpile, supplier concentration, enforceability): what matters is whether withholding an input collapses the network, not how much of it is imported. Part I supplies an answer that is architecture- and parameter-conditioned, and must be stated that way: where import dependency remains load-bearing — the lunar and Mars ensembles — electronics is the most frequently observed minimum cut, and the vitamin parts are the last governance handle; in the sampled central LEO draws a single import line is fatal only in a minority of draws (~0–11% for electronics across r3.5 regenerations), so there the material handle runs mainly through feed availability and the recovery loop rather than any one import line. Material-dependency governance has a declining window as closure rises; throttle authority, verification requirements, and replication limits are cheapest to embed as licensing conditions during the bridge period. Closure does not end governance (communications, cryptographic authority, personnel, ownership, insurance, treaties, orbital traffic, trade remain), but the material handle is the one that closes.

A serious control architecture — beyond this document’s scope but within its obligation to specify — requires hardware roots of authority; multi-party authorization for replication steps; explicit rate limits; compartmentalization and fault-containment zones; secure update and rollback channels; explicit cyberattack and insider-risk models; independent verification capacity; operator and government incentive analysis; jurisdiction and liability rules; and failure recovery when communications with Earth are unavailable. These are named requirements, not solved problems. They must be tested under the same loss-of-import, shock, and degraded-communications conditions used to test the production system.

Design principle: replication authority should be the most verified and least autonomous function in the system — positioned deliberately against the efficiency gradient, embedded at the seed stage, because it cannot be retrofitted onto a mature complex.

The ranking above asks which observations discriminate among H0, H1, and H2. A second set asks whether the physical thesis is becoming feasible at all. These lower-diagnosticity but still necessary indicators from the Second Edition are retained:

  • robots performing unscripted maintenance, construction, mining, and machine tending for long periods, with autonomous recovery fraction and human intervention hours reported per machine-hour rather than described only as “limited”;
  • local production of propellant, structural material, and replacement parts at operational rather than laboratory scale;
  • factories using locally processed feedstock to manufacture additional power, extraction, and production equipment;
  • a measured decline in imported mass per unit of local productive capacity, reported by component class rather than as an aggregate mass fraction;
  • element-level closure reported as inputs, inventories, losses, recycled flows, and source-specific yields—not inferred from aggregate local mass;
  • space-manufactured compute, or production equipment capable of making it;
  • net positive industrial reproduction after depreciation — P > D in every indispensable sector — with published r and δ estimates by component class;
  • the Terafab-specific milestones in III.2: first wafer and yield, process equipment orders, in-house memory and packaging, qualified D3 silicon, orbital-compute revenue, and a measured robot reproduction ratio;
  • open-weight systems demonstrating sustained autonomous verified improvement loops — evidence for the threshold-like branch in II.5 — and, separately, fleet- and factory-owning actors outperforming open models on engineering tasks in a way attributable to proprietary process data; the contrary result would show that κ_D does not bind;
  • a sustained measured decline in cost per verified improvement loop, and replication-relevant conditions appearing in launch licences or export-control terms while material leverage still exists.

Threshold observations that would move Appendix A: a demonstrated ISRU plant above ~50% full-system materials closure; audited robot production figures; an independent satellite-reliability audit reporting cohort-specific failures per satellite-year, separated from retirements and planned disposals (the earlier “~2.5–3%” figure was an early-cohort fleet fraction, not a general hazard rate, so divergence from it is not the right test); and any traceable datum on off-world-producible electronics tiers — the vitamin-gap anchor this document flags as its highest-value currently parameterized missing measurement. The thesis weakens if humanoid robotics remains economically narrow, advanced manufacturing cannot be localized, launch costs stop declining, closure requires a permanently growing Earth supply chain, verification costs rise faster than capacity — or if Terafab devolves into design-and-packaging dependent on external front-end fabrication, which would reopen the fabrication term entirely.

III.6 Refined complete thesis

The coherent end state remains a recursively self-expanding space industrial ecology. Reusable rockets transport an initial seed. AI supplies scientific, engineering, and coordinating intelligence. Robots provide scalable physical labor. Local energy systems supply power. Mining and refining convert extraterrestrial matter into industrial feedstocks. Automated factories produce structures, machines, robots, transport vehicles, energy systems, and — if the electronics tier can close — compute. Communications integrate specialized nodes into one economic and operational network.

The network first reduces Earth dependence, then reaches self-sustenance, and finally produces net additional capacity. At high closure, station-keeping, collision management, maintenance, transport, and coordination remain real requirements; they are supplied by industries inside the network rather than purchased as externally scarce services.

matter + energy + intelligence + time → expanding productive capacity

Recursive self-improvement is therefore not limited to software. Intelligence improves the machinery and institutions that create intelligence; the industrial substrate returns energy, compute, data, experimentation, and physical agency to the intelligence layer. The ultimate product is an industrial system capable of producing more of itself.

The most precise formulation now available is conditional and model-separated. Current evidence substantially supports deliberate integrated space–AI–industrial development (P4a), but not an intended transition to recursive closure (P4b). The inherited industrial model finds expansion under some assumption sets and failure under others, with electronics and feedstock dependencies localized by architecture. The new integrated-bootstrap model makes a first explicit pass through the complete intelligence–industry loop, seed bill of material, source network, and second-cell commissioning; it finds the lunar vitamin bridge and the composite Moon–asteroid scenario feasible under central assumptions and strict lunar closure infeasible. Because every new structural coefficient is declared rather than measured, that is an executable hypothesis, not evidence that the complete recursion can be built. The comparative-economics model finds the Earth-dependent bridge uneconomic at anchored values and does not price the mature high-closure ecology. Capability accumulation is observed; positive off-world industrial reproduction and a unified closure strategy are not.

I(t+1)=I(t)+f(C,D,V,H),H(t+1)=H(t)+g(I,E,M,R)−δenvH(t),C(t+1)=C(t)+h(H,E,fab) \begin{aligned} I(t+1) &= I(t) + f(C,D,V,H), \\ H(t+1) &= H(t) + g(I,E,M,R) - \delta_{\mathrm{env}}H(t), \\ C(t+1) &= C(t) + h(H,E,\mathrm{fab}) \end{aligned}

In earlier editions f, g, and h were unspecified placeholders and this compact model was a causal diagram. The inherited model operationalizes g as the lexicographic maximin allocation of Part I.7 (with repair, recycling, stockpiles, and imports) and decomposes δ_env by sector and environment. The Fourth layer supplies a first stylized f and h: installed compute drives verified intelligence through a saturating gain law; intelligence raises task autonomy and industrial productivity; the resulting operating cells manufacture additional compute after construction and commissioning lags. Constant-rate Earth-fed deployment is run beside it as the counterfactual. These functions are executable and swept, but their coefficients are ASSUMPTION-class and they omit training data, algorithmic regime changes, verification failures, hardware-node detail, and many coordination limits. The model therefore tests the consequences of a coupling law; it does not measure that law or establish takeoff, subject also to the speed caveat of II.5.

What the thesis does not claim

  • It does not establish that the companies are managed under one confidential master plan; III.3 assesses the public evidence.
  • It does not imply that energy, matter, infrastructure, coordination, or opportunity cost become literally free.
  • It does not assume intelligence automatically solves semiconductor fabrication, reliability, verification, governance, or safety.
  • It does not claim that the degrees of freedom of a current humanoid robot demonstrate unscripted industrial autonomy; embodied task coverage, recovery, and verification remain unmeasured requirements.
  • It does not require human settlement, agriculture, or a closed biosphere for the reference machine ecology, nor does it claim humans could never be present. A crewed architecture is a larger closure problem than the one modeled here.
  • It does not require every industrial node to be self-contained; closure can exist at network level.
  • It does not require uninterrupted exponential growth; the system can move through bottleneck-limited regimes and can double before later losing viability.
  • It does not claim Terafab will achieve viable yields or off-world fabrication closure; it is a declared terrestrial attempt whose implementation remains unsettled.
  • It does not claim a slow takeoff is certain; the physical-path thesis is conditional on the slow-to-medium branch in II.5.
  • It does not claim that closure is imminent, that model frequencies are empirical probabilities, or that space compute is economical today.

Part IV — Conclusion, and Speculative Terminal Scenarios

IV.1 The defensible core, restated with numbers attached

Two walls, two escapes, one passage. The capital wall yields to expected returns inside the historical envelope; the operating wall binds through politics rather than physics at current scale; both escapes remain conditional on surviving the bridge — and the bridge’s failure mode is now specific: hold electronics-class stockpiles against shelf life, and reach local production of some electronics tier before the import ramp decays. The defensible core conclusion stops here, with its conditionals quantified: if industrial closure becomes sufficiently high across every indispensable sector (Part I’s bottleneck is architecture-specific: capture/mining dominates LEO, electronics dominates the Moon, and electronics and power co-bind on Mars) and terrestrial expansion costs rise faster than off-world deployment costs (Part II says: not at anchored values; only in the qualification/life/utilization corner at target-class launch prices), then some large-scale industrial and computational activity may migrate off Earth. Everything beyond that sentence is scenario.

IV.2 After convergence: deployment as the terminal regime

At some point the marginal value of further design improvement falls below the marginal value of another deployed node — architectural convergence. Past it, intelligence behaves as a commodity produced by infrastructure — heuristically, throughput ~ deployed energy × physical efficiency, a relation that omits memory, interconnect, utilization, data, reliability, verification, latency, workload composition, and fabrication yield. Efficiency is floor-bounded (Landauer, with reversible logic the stated exception); scale plausibly becomes the dominant marginal variable rather than the only one; scale means energy; energy means collector area; and collector area is not bounded by a planetary surface off Earth. It remains bounded — by matter, transport, orbital geometry, heat transport, collision risk, maintenance, and control, and per the published cluster analysis by the quadratic scaling of satellite count with cluster radius and by mutual occlusion of rejected infrared between closely flown spacecraft [S32]. The ceilings-and-rates argument is retained in its r2.10 sensitivity form: the waste-heat ceiling sits perhaps two orders above current civilizational energy use, the thermodynamic floor several orders below current efficiency, and the ~3×/yr algorithmic-efficiency figure [S26] measures compute-for-fixed-language-performance only. Under sustained compounding, distances that look like centuries become decades — whether the compounding is sustained is precisely what is unknown.

The two regimes can be stated as marginal cost curves. On Earth, the marginal cost of the n-th node can rise as the operator bids up land, congests grids, and encounters political and thermal limits. In space, after closure, the marginal cost can fall through learning and self-replication. One curve rises and the other falls; their crossover is the migration point, and it moves earlier as launch cost declines, closure improves, equipment life lengthens, or qualification premiums fall. Part II shows that the curves do not cross at the anchored bridge-economy values.

Model check. The cost-curve claim — Earth’s marginal node rises with n, space’s falls with n after closure — is the crossover surface, and at anchored values the curves do not cross. The scenario needs three demanding unanchored conditions: rad-tolerance at commodity cost (the strongest current data supports five-year TID survivability but leaves system reliability — SEE, training workloads, thermal, whole-spacecraft — unresolved [S32]), service life nearer 15 years than 5, and target-class launch or closure substituting for launch. The collector-area arithmetic is not the disputed part; the three demanding unanchored conditions are where the scenario lives.

Two conditions attach. Demand saturation: if the set of tasks worth computing is bounded, demand saturates and the argument that space is the only arena permitting unbounded deployment loses its motivation [S34]. The physical ceilings bound how cheaply computation can be done; a demand ceiling would bound how much is wanted — the more decisive and less examined of the two. The non-saturation branch has a canonical published statement [S35] and no resolution; the scenario is conditional on it. The upgrade asymmetry, narrowed: software-layer improvements propagate to deployed compute at near-zero marginal cost, but hardware-dependent gains require replacement or retrofit — silicon is not upgraded in place. The model consequence: the space-electronics obsolescence term would shrink after convergence only for its software-limited share.

IV.3 Paradigm shifts: delay, alteration, or prevention

Catch-up time (a new paradigm must beat the incumbent plus decades of compounded process learning, still improving as the newcomer matures) and finiteness (irreversible-computation paradigms climb toward the same ceiling; reversible computing is the stated exception) bound the leapfrog objection. Exposure is concentrated in fabrication on 10–20-year horizons; energy, labor, transport, and materials are less paradigm-sensitive — substantially reusable, not immune. The fleet self-insures through its own 3–6-year depreciation; the fab carries the stranding risk — the penalty of taking the lead.

General-purpose robotic manufacturing is a partial hedge because it is the industry that builds industries: after a paradigm shift, the cheapest retrofitter may be the actor able to build the next fab faster than a rival builds its first. The corresponding strategy is to verticalize the less paradigm-sensitive layers aggressively, preserve hardware generality and retooling capacity at the paradigm-specific layer, and track retooling speed alongside doubling time. The model check below shows that this hedge must be pre-positioned; it cannot be improvised after a catastrophic electronics loss.

Model check. The paradigm-sensitivity split maps onto the model’s sector structure: a shift perturbs the electronics column of the requirement matrix and the fab layer behind the vitamin imports — exactly the sector already identified as binding in the relevant architectures. Machine tools and robotics are necessary retooling capabilities, but all eight modeled physical production sectors are knockout-indispensable; the knockout experiment does not show that those two are uniquely more strategically valuable. Retooling speed is a computed output (cutsets.retooling_time), and it hardens this scenario’s central risk into a result: destroying 90% of the electronics or machine-tools sector at any point on the central trajectory is model-fatal — a defined, horizon-relative label: worst-case growth has not returned positive within the tested 20-year post-knockout window while the unknocked counterfactual’s stays positive, under both the myopic and receding-horizon allocators, and at optimistic values too. (r3.5 correction of r3.4’s “fatal, not a delay — permanently negative”: the computation tests a 20-year horizon under the stated deterministic configuration; it establishes no recovery within that window, not permanent impossibility — a longer horizon, resumed imports, altered plant design, larger initial surplus, or a lower knock fraction could change the outcome and are unswept.) The mechanism is the vitamin gap: at electronics q-scale 0.10–0.30, replacement local capacity is a multi-decade-payback asset no plausible planning horizon rebuilds from current output. Only power stranding under optimistic values is a finite delay (~27 years lost). Conditional implication: within this scenario class, a paradigm shift that obsoletes the local electronics tier removes the self-expansion margin for at least a two-decade window unless Earth imports resume — so the leapfrog hedge must be pre-positioned (hardware generality, retooling-ready lines, retained umbilical capacity), because post-shift rebuild from inside the complex is not a path the model offers within any tested horizon. Caveats: deterministic values, 90% single-sector knockout, maximin allocation family, 20-year window; surplus-pinning (stage 3), knock-fraction sweeps, and longer-horizon recovery tests remain open follow-ups.

IV.4 The alternative telos, and the properly conditional close

One reading of the same physics reaches this document’s destination from opposite values: Earth is not a constrained platform to escape but a uniquely valuable biosphere to protect — zone the planet for habitation, site heavy industry and compute where there is no ecology to disturb [S34]. Independent corroboration of the operating-wall mechanism comes from the same quarter: data-center siting is politically contested over electricity, water, and land. The physical argument is common to both readings; only the telos differs — and this decouples P3 from P4: the migration case can be made without any claim about one company’s intentions.

The observed portfolio is consistent with an economic gradient toward greater integration — discovery until convergence, deployment after it, with space as one plausible arena under a demanding set of crossover conditions that this edition has now priced. Present evidence does not establish that recursive off-world industrial closure is the governing objective of the companies described in Part III. Earlier editions ended by saying a model could decide; this one built the model. What the model says is: the recursion is feasible only under assumptions that are currently choices rather than measurements; the economics point to Earth at every anchored value; and the observations that would change either conclusion are now named, ranked, and waiting.

Limitations and claim boundary

The most robust outputs are structural within the implemented model: balanced doubling is unit-invariant; the three-stage allocator has a unique state transition; realized endpoints include same-step shocks; recoverable scrap cannot exceed loss-specific retirement; and Part II is exactly affine in launch price. Architecture comparisons, endpoint definitions, and regeneration from fixed seeds are also auditable.

The substantive conclusions remain assumption-conditioned. In the inherited model, the requirement matrix, homogeneous electronics-closure multiplier, repair and recovery efficiencies, stockpiles, shock process, import profile, and finite capture reservoir are not measured as a coupled industrial system. Its scalar-anchor screen does not rank structural matrix uncertainty, interactions, policy families, or all environments, and its LEO model has neither an endogenous debris stock nor a depleted capture reservoir.

The Fourth layer adds an element-by-sector bill of materials, source yields, Earth–Moon–orbit–asteroid transfer arcs, seed-mass optimization, four knowledge layers, task-resolved autonomy, intervention demand, process-specific gravity and centrifuge overhead, construction and transport lags, commissioning, yield ramps, minimum viable scale, a second production cell, and an active/derelict/fragment debris state. These are model features, not validated factories. Its bills of material, recoverable yields, source throughput, autonomy/productivity gains, lag and ramp coefficients, intervention budget, asteroid portfolio, transfer capacities, and collision kernels are declared assumptions. The seed is minimum-mass only inside its fixed linear architecture. Seventeen material categories do not resolve mineralogy, alloying routes, contamination, process chemistry, purity ladders, tool wear, quality yield, or every consumable. The electronics tiers remain aggregates rather than fabs and tool chains. Documented elemental occurrence is not a grade distribution, recoverable reserve, refining yield, or accessible asteroid portfolio.

The terrestrial rehearsal in I.9 remains a proposed validation protocol, not an accomplished demonstration; its computed scenario is a requirements case, not an experiment. Gravity is treated more faithfully than a location label, but only through declared process compatibility and centrifuge mass/power penalties; the model does not simulate granular mechanics, convection, melt behavior, vacuum welding, dust, radiation, traction, or thermal cycling. The compute–intelligence loop is normalized and phenomenological. The Earth counterfactual imposes rate and siting ceilings rather than deriving land, grid, water, permitting, labor, or capital prices. The debris module is a stress-test stock-flow model, not a shell-resolved conjunction or traffic simulation.

Part II omits communications and ground-network capex, program NRE, insurance, spectrum and regulatory costs, and several spacecraft-bus functions; serviceability is a scenario, not an observed property. The Part II cost comparison is not dynamically coupled to the Fourth layer, so P3b remains unpriced even where the physical-model quantities compound. Several inherited Part II intervals still resolve through the collective [S36] record rather than a complete claim-level bibliography. That is sufficient to reproduce the computation, not to independently audit every range endpoint; Appendix A marks the affected calibration as provisional. Finally, the Part III strategy assessment is ordinal and observational: corporate filings and statements establish declared initiatives, not an intent to achieve recursive closure. No result in this paper is a forecast or an empirical probability of success.

D.1 Fourth Edition integrated-bootstrap layer

State space. The implementation has 18 sectors: power, mining, refining, robotics, machine tools, construction, thermal, transport, repair, recycling, interconnects/boards, passives/power, sensors/control, packaging/test, mature logic, advanced logic/memory, process equipment/metrology, and compute. The full coefficient set is the machine-readable specification in params/fourth_edition.yaml.

Element and service closure. For element ee, sector jj, and source/transfer arc aa:

ΔMe=∑sYs,eXs+Ie+∑aFa,ein−∑jBe,jxj−Le−∑aFa,eout, \Delta M_e = \sum_s Y_{s,e}X_s + I_e + \sum_a F^{\mathrm{in}}_{a,e} - \sum_j B_{e,j}x_j - L_e - \sum_a F^{\mathrm{out}}_{a,e},

where Be,jB_{e,j} is the amount of element ee required per unit of sector-jj build, Ys,eY_{s,e} is the recoverable yield from source and process ss, and Fa,eF_{a,e} is element flow across an Earth, lunar surface, orbital, or asteroid transfer arc. Concentration alone does not set YY; mineral form, beneficiation, refining energy, purity, and loss remain outside the occurrence citations. Service closure uses Ai,jA_{i,j}, the sector-ii service output needed to reproduce and replace sector jj. Recoverable lunar and asteroid yields, rather than bulk inventory, enter the constraints. Earth material appears only as the bounded initial vitamin stock vev_e.

Process environment and autonomy. For each process jj, task coverage at AI level II is

cj(I)=min⁡{cjmax,cj0+αjmax⁡(I−1,0)}. c_j(I)=\min\{c_j^{\max},\ c_j^0+\alpha_j\max(I-1,0)\}.

Effective productivity is multiplied by (0.5+0.5cj)(0.5+0.5c_j). Residual annual intervention is hj(1−cj)zjh_j(1-c_j)z_j and its sum must fit the scenario budget. Where declared required acceleration gj*g_j^\ast exceeds lunar gravity gL=0.165gg_L=0.165g, the acceleration gap adds centrifuge structural material and parasitic power. The reported centrifuge speed follows

rpmj=602π9.80665max⁡(gj*−gL,0)rj. \mathrm{rpm}_j=\frac{60}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{9.80665\max(g_j^\ast-g_L,0)}{r_j}}.

This is an equipment penalty, not a claim that a whole lunar factory or habitat requires artificial gravity.

Seed sizing. Decision variables are sector capacities zjz_j, annual lunar and asteroid feed pL,pAp_L,p_A, and initial Earth vitamins vev_e. The linear program minimizes

Mseed=Mdigital+∑jzj∑eBe,jeff+∑eve, M_{\mathrm{seed}} = M_{\mathrm{digital}}+\sum_j z_j\sum_e B_{e,j}^{\mathrm{eff}} +\sum_e v_e,

subject to minimum viable sector capacities, task coverage and intervention limits, service output sufficient for replacement plus one complete second cell, element balances through each scenario horizon, and mining, refining, transport, and energy throughput. Sector commissioning lags and half-ramp loss reduce productive years. Mdigital=5M_{\mathrm{digital}}=5 t represents the software, simulation, industrial-memory, and experimental package; its value is an assumption, not a storage-mass estimate.

Dynamic reproduction. A feasible seed begins as one installed and operating cell. Each quarter, lunar production, commissioned asteroid production, and recovered retirement enter inventory; the complete cell-level material vector limits new orders. Orders become installed after a two-year construction queue and operating through a 1.5-year yield ramp. Cells depreciate at 0.04/yr and 82% of retired cell material is assumed recoverable. The first time operating cells reach 2.0 is the explicit second-cell result.

Installed compute CtC_t raises normalized intelligence according to

It+Δt=min⁡{Imax,It+ℓvlog⁡(1+Ct/C0)Δt}, I_{t+\Delta t} = \min\{I_{\max}, I_t +\ell\,v\log(1+C_t/C_0)\Delta t\},

where ℓ\ell is the learning coefficient and vv the verification factor. Intelligence above the scenario starting level raises both industrial productivity and autonomy, which raises cell output and future compute. Earth and Earth-fed orbital counterfactuals instead add capacity at constant, scenario-specific build/delivery rates, subtract 0.16/yr depreciation, and impose terrestrial site ceilings. Moon→orbit compute has a 0.5-year lag, a 35% share of new endogenous capacity, and a transport-capacity ceiling.

Debris state. A separate stress test evolves active assets, derelicts, and a fragment index. Failures, controlled disposal, retrieval, collisions, fragment creation, natural clearance, and active removal are explicit. RdebrisR_{\mathrm{debris}} is fragments created divided by fragments cleared over the last five years. The distributed case multiplies the collision kernel by 0.01; it is therefore a declared topology test, not a derived property of heliocentric space.

Scenarios and uncertainty. The four named cases are terrestrial rehearsal, lunar vitamin bridge, strict lunar-only, and Moon–asteroid network. Their horizons, AI levels, import caps, intervention budgets, and source commissioning dates are all explicit in the YAML. Each 200-run ensemble uses stable crc32 names from seed 404 to draw independent log-uniform multipliers for service productivity, bills of material, lunar and asteroid yield, depreciation, lag, and autonomy gap. Frequencies across those draws are not empirical probabilities. D.2 Inherited ten-sector model

Sectors and units. n = 10 sectors, ordered: power, mining, refining, robotics, machine_tools, electronics, thermal, transport, repair, recycling. Capacity K_j is in abstract capacity units (K_j(0) = 1 for all j unless stated); sector j produces q_j output units per capacity unit per year (q below is the central set after environment q-scaling for the lunar architecture is applied via environments.yaml; the tabulated q is the pre-scaling set value). Repair and recycling are service sectors: their rows in A are zero, and their outputs are consumed as described below.

Time. Discrete steps of Δt = 0.25 yr (0.5 yr for cut-set enumeration), default horizon 35–40 yr. This inherited layer has no construction lags, commissioning delays, yield ramps, or minimum efficient scales — all its outputs are stylized model outputs, not engineering timescale forecasts.

Imports. m_i(t) = B₀ · e^(−t/τ) · φ_i, with per-sector cutoffs (m_i := 0 for i in the cut set after the cutoff year). Stockable sectors’ imports flow into the stockpile; non-stockable flow into the current balance. Mars: stockable imports arrive as synodic batches of size m·(2.14 yr) every 2.14 yr.

Allocation (per step): three-stage lexicographic optimization. Variables: builds x ∈ ℝⁿ₊, wear-repair u ∈ ℝⁿ₊, recycling activity w ∈ ℝ₊, stockpile drawdown d ∈ ℝⁿ₊ (d_i = 0 for non-stockable i), growth floor g ∈ ℝ.

Stage 1 — maximize g subject to:

xj+uj−δjKj≥gKj∀j,uj≤δwear,jKj∀j,∑jAijxj+sf∑jAijuj+𝟏i=repair1ηrep∑juj+𝟏i=recyclingw+oiw−ρiw−di≤qiKi+mdirect,i∀i,w+ηscrap∑jfwear,juj≤ηscrap∑j,cfc,jδc,jKj,di≤SiΔt. \begin{aligned} x_j + u_j - \delta_j K_j &\ge gK_j && \forall j, \\ u_j &\le \delta_{\mathrm{wear},j}K_j && \forall j, \\ \sum_j A_{ij}x_j + s_f\sum_j A_{ij}u_j + \mathbf{1}_{i=\mathrm{repair}}\frac{1}{\eta_{\mathrm{rep}}}\sum_j u_j + \mathbf{1}_{i=\mathrm{recycling}}w + o_iw-\rho_iw-d_i &\le q_iK_i+m_{\mathrm{direct},i} && \forall i, \\ w+\eta_{\mathrm{scrap}}\sum_j f_{\mathrm{wear},j}u_j &\le \eta_{\mathrm{scrap}} \sum_{j,c}f_{c,j}\delta_{c,j}K_j, \\ d_i &\le \frac{S_i}{\Delta t}. \end{aligned}

with s_f = spares fraction (0.15), η_rep = repair efficiency (0.75 central), η_scrap = processing yield (0.40 central, < 1 always), o = recycling opex vector, ρ = recycling output profile, and f_c,j = the recoverable share of loss component c in sector j. R(K,u)=∑j,cfc,jδc,jKj−∑jfwear,juj. R(K,u)=\sum_{j,c}f_{c,j}\delta_{c,j}K_j -\sum_j f_{\mathrm{wear},j}u_j.

Stage 2 holds g at its optimum and minimizes dimensionless stock draw ∑iωidi\sum_i\omega_i d_i, where ωi\omega_i is the inverse of a same-unit scale based on current supply and available stock, normalized by the largest weight. Stock is a buffer, not fuel, and the objective is invariant to changes in sector row units.

Stage 3 holds the stage-1 and stage-2 optima within solver tolerance and minimizes a strictly convex normalized action norm:

min⁡[∑jxj2+uj2K̂j2+w2R̂2+∑idi2D̂i2+g2]. \min\left[ \sum_j\frac{x_j^2+u_j^2}{\widehat K_j^2} +\frac{w^2}{\widehat R^2} +\sum_i\frac{d_i^2}{\widehat D_i^2} +g^2\right].

All scales are positive state-derived quantities with numerical floors. The positive-definite Hessian makes the selected action, and therefore the state transition, unique. Stage 1 and 2 use HiGHS; stage 3 uses OSQP with a public SLSQP fallback for rare narrow-face numerical failures.

State updates and shocks.

Kj←max⁡(Kj+Δt(xj+uj−δjKj),10−9),Si←clip⁡(Si+inflowi−diΔt−SiΔtLi,0,Ci). \begin{aligned} K_j &\leftarrow \max\!\left(K_j+\Delta t\,(x_j+u_j-\delta_jK_j),10^{-9}\right), \\ S_i &\leftarrow \operatorname{clip}\!\left( S_i+\mathrm{inflow}_i-d_i\Delta t-\frac{S_i\Delta t}{L_i}, 0,C_i \right). \end{aligned}

while the allocator’s pre-shock g*g^* is retained separately as g_alloc.

Glossary

Recursive self-improvement (RSI): a process in which an intelligent system contributes to producing a more capable successor.

Industrial closure (κ): the degree to which a production network internally supplies the critical inputs required for its own operation. The proper object is a sector vector with criticality and substitutability, not a single mass fraction.

Closure engineering: the quantitative discipline, originating in the 1980 NASA/ASEE summer study, of accounting for every input a replicating industrial system must produce or import.

Self-sustaining: able to replace consumed and depreciated productive capacity without an essential external supply.

Self-expanding: able to produce net additional productive capacity after replacement, repair, and losses.

Industrial seed: a compact initial system intended to grow into a broader local production network. In this edition it has four layers: physical equipment; digital models and toolchains; industrial memory; and experimental capacity for generating missing knowledge.

Digital seed: the AI weights, firmware, controllers, compilers, CAD/CAM, simulators, optimization and verification tools, and locally available software dependencies transported with an industrial seed.

Industrial memory: the machine-readable recipes, material and equipment models, calibration and maintenance procedures, build records, failure histories, intervention traces, and provenance required to operate, repair, and adapt the physical seed.

Embodied-autonomy threshold: the task coverage and recovery capability at which a robot-and-AI system can perform the unscripted diagnosis, tool use, construction, repair, learning, and verification needed to keep the industrial graph operating without routine human intervention. It is not a joint-count or robot-count threshold.

Terrestrial autonomous-closure rehearsal: an Earth-based test of the candidate off-world seed under constrained materials, energy, communications, spares, and human access, culminating in construction and commissioning of a second production cell.

Exogenous deployment: capacity growth supplied by an external industrial base, such as Earth-manufactured data centers delivered to orbit.

Endogenous industrial growth: capacity growth produced from within the industrial network by allocating part of its output to reproducing and expanding productive equipment.

Material vitamins: low-mass, high-criticality inputs—such as advanced chips, dopants, catalysts, specialty chemicals, sensors, bearings, or precision tooling—whose absence can stop a high-mass locally supplied industry.

Process-specific artificial gravity: centrifugal acceleration supplied to a particular machine or production module when microgravity or lunar gravity does not meet that process’s requirements; distinct from rotating an entire habitat or industrial complex.

Clock-setting component: the component with the shortest effective replacement horizon that cannot yet be produced, repaired, substituted, or stockpiled locally; it bounds the survival time of the partially closed system.

Replication time: the time a production network needs to reproduce a unit of productive capacity. It is unknown as a measurement here and appears only as a set-labeled model-output distribution.

Bridge economy: the sequence of independently financeable products and services that carries the system through the capital-intensive stages before closure.

ISRU: in-situ resource utilization — obtaining and using materials at the mission destination rather than importing every supply from Earth.

Productive throughput: the rate at which a system converts energy, materials, compute, and machine time into useful output.

Distributed industrial ecology: a network of specialized, mutually dependent production nodes whose combined input-output graph is self-sustaining or self-expanding.

Scarcity becomes latency: the proposition that, after high closure, the principal limit on many outputs becomes production allocation and completion time rather than external purchase price. Opportunity cost remains.

Von Neumann replicator: a machine system capable of constructing a functional copy of itself from available materials; the formal category to which a fully closed industrial complex belongs.

Terafab: the chip-fabrication initiative announced in March 2026 by Tesla, SpaceX, and xAI and joined by Intel in April, targeting advanced AI chips including radiation-tolerant orbital silicon. The SEC-filed offering materials describe a general framework with unsettled implementation, not a demonstrated fab or legally constituted joint venture [S27].

Society-priced costs: operating inputs priced substantially through human institutions rather than physical conversion alone — land, permitting, interconnection, litigation, and political consent. Space relocates these costs toward gateways such as launch, spectrum, liability, and treaty rather than abolishing them.

Threshold-model argument: the proposition that once a base model crosses the capability needed to run verified improvement loops, a smarter starting model may confer only a transient head start and durable advantage may shift toward hardware doubling time and industrial closure. This is the small-α branch of II.5, not an established law.

Architectural convergence: the point at which the marginal value of another design improvement falls below that of another deployed node. Beyond it, growth is increasingly deployment-limited.

Paradigm-sensitivity split: the division between less paradigm-sensitive layers — energy, labor, transport, and bulk materials — and the more paradigm-specific fabrication layer, where stranding and leapfrog risk concentrate.

Retooling speed: the time a manufacturing complex needs to reconfigure for a new technological paradigm. Alongside hardware doubling time, it is a first-class competitive and resilience metric.

Physical-process data: training-relevant data generated by operating physical industry — telemetry, process settings, intervention records, failures, and fleet or manipulation traces.

Data-advantage index (κ_D): an index over uniqueness, causal linkage, coverage, quality, accessibility, integration, and verification value. Total training-relevant data has no measurable denominator, so κ_D is not a closure ratio.

Unified build record: a per-unit data structure linking design intent (as designed), production parameters (as built), and operational telemetry (as deployed), making physical-process data causally trainable.

Vitamin parts: the residual imported microelectronics, bearings, precision instruments, catalysts, or other high-complexity inputs that resist local production. The term comes from the 1980 closure literature.

Vitamin gap: in this model, the unclosable fraction of the electronics sector’s output mix — the results-driving unknown sampled through the space electronics q-scale.

Coasean boundary: the organizational boundary at which internal coordination costs equal market transaction costs. AI can lower both sides, so the direction in which the efficient boundary moves is empirical.

Gateway leverage: enforceable governance authority concentrated at dependencies such as launch, spectrum, export control, finance, communications, cryptographic authority, and trade. Its material component follows criticality, substitutability, stockpile, supplier concentration, and enforceability — not the scalar expression (1 − κ).

Minimum cut set: a smallest combination of imported inputs whose loss prevents self-expansion.

Likelihood ratio of evidence: how much more expected an observation is under one hypothesis than under a competitor. Part III uses ordinal diagnosticity rather than numerical multiplication because evidence items are correlated and observability is uncertain.

Maximin growth rate g: the worst-case sector growth rate maximized by the allocation program, ensuring that aggregate growth cannot hide collapse in an indispensable sector.

Assumption set: a named, complete collection of ASSUMPTION-class structural coefficients used together in a model run.

Anchor: a published, source-classed range sampled by Monte Carlo; an anchor constrains an input without converting it into a measurement of the whole system.

Computed: derived mechanically from stated assumptions by the companion model. A computed result inherits the epistemic class of its weakest input.

Kessler syndrome / collisional cascade: an endogenous orbital-debris feedback in which collisions create fragments that increase the probability of later collisions. It is not equivalent to a fixed independent debris hazard.

Sun-synchronous orbit (SSO): a planet-centered precessing orbit selected so that the spacecraft passes a reference point at approximately the same local solar time. Earth SSO is ordinarily a LEO architecture; it does not mean an orbit around the Sun.

Heliocentric orbit: an orbit around the Sun. In the post-lunar scenario, “distributed heliocentric” means separated orbital elements and resource trajectories rather than a new single dense shell.
